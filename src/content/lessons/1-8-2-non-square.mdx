## Question

Every matrix so far was square: 2 by 2 or 3 by 3. What could a matrix with 3 rows and 2 columns do? Or 1 row and 2 columns?

## Numbers

The 3 by 2 matrix has two columns, one for <span class="ihat">**î**</span> and one for <span class="jhat">**ĵ**</span>. Each column has three numbers, because each one lands in 3D:

<Formula display tex="\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ -1 & 1 \\ -2 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = 1 \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ -1 \\ -2 \end{bmatrix} + 1 \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 0 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix}" />

The 1 by 2 matrix also has two columns, but each column is just one number: <span class="ihat">**î**</span> lands on 1 and <span class="jhat">**ĵ**</span> lands on −2.

<Formula display tex="\begin{bmatrix} 1 & -2 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 4 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix} = 4 \cdot 1 + 3 \cdot (-2) = -2" />

## Explain

- **Columns = how many numbers go in.** <Formula tex="A\mathbf{x}" /> needs one number of <Formula tex="\mathbf{x}" /> per column (Lesson 1.4.1). 2 columns means the input is 2D.
- **Rows = how many numbers come out.** Each column has as many numbers as <Formula tex="A" /> has rows, and so does every mix of them. 3 rows means the output is 3D.
- **Same rule as always.** Take <Formula tex="x" /> copies of column 1 plus <Formula tex="y" /> copies of column 2. Nothing about "mix the columns" needs the matrix to be square.

**The words for it.** The rule <Formula tex="T(\mathbf{x}) = A\mathbf{x}" /> for an <Formula tex="m \times n" /> matrix is a *matrix transformation* <Formula tex="T : \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^m" />. <Formula tex="\mathbb{R}^n" /> (where the inputs live) is its *domain*, and <Formula tex="\mathbb{R}^m" /> (where the outputs live) is its *codomain*. <Formula tex="T(\mathbf{x})" /> is the *image* of <Formula tex="\mathbf{x}" />, and the set of all images is the *range* of <Formula tex="T" />.

- **The range of a 3 by 2 matrix is only a plane.** Every output is a mix of the two columns, so the range is their span (Lesson 1.3.3): a flat sheet through the origin, not all of the codomain <Formula tex="\mathbb{R}^3" />. "Is <Formula tex="\mathbf{b}" /> in the range?" is the same question as "does <Formula tex="A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}" /> have a solution?"
- **A 1 by 2 matrix squashes the plane onto a line.** Its range is a number line, and a whole line of inputs lands on each number.

## Predict

Which points of the plane land on **0** on the number line under <Formula tex="\begin{bmatrix} 1 & -2 \end{bmatrix}" />?

<details>
  <summary>Reveal</summary>
  All points with <Formula tex="x - 2y = 0" />, which is the line <Formula tex="y = x / 2" /> through <Formula tex="(0, 0)" />, <Formula tex="(2, 1)" /> and <Formula tex="(4, 2)" />. A whole line of points gets squashed onto one number, so this can't be undone: from the number 0 alone you can't tell which point it came from. §1.9 gives this a name (not *one-to-one*). Try dragging <span class="vec">**v**</span> to <Formula tex="(2, 1)" /> and pressing Play.

## Go deeper

A matrix with <Formula tex="m" /> rows and <Formula tex="n" /> columns (an "<Formula tex="m \times n" /> matrix") takes <Formula tex="n" />-dimensional vectors to <Formula tex="m" />-dimensional ones. Rows first, then columns, the same order as a seat number in a theatre.

To multiply two matrices, you do one move and then the other, so the sizes have to fit: the output of the first must be the input of the second. A <Formula tex="1 \times 3" /> matrix can follow a <Formula tex="3 \times 2" /> one (2D to 3D, then 3D to a number), and the product is <Formula tex="1 \times 2" />. Try to explain why a <Formula tex="3 \times 2" /> matrix can't follow another <Formula tex="3 \times 2" /> one.
