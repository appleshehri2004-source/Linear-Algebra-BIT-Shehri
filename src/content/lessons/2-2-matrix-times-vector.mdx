## Question

A linear transformation moves *every* point of the plane. Do you really need to know where every point goes, or is a little information enough?

## Play

Press **Play**. Then drag the tips of <span class="ihat">**î**</span>, <span class="jhat">**ĵ**</span> and <span class="vec">**v**</span>, or type new numbers into the matrix.
The line under the matrix shows where <span class="vec">**v**</span> lands, built from the columns.

<MatrixPlayground client:visible matrix={[[1, -2], [3, 0]]} tracked={[-1, 2]} showCombination />

Notice: <span class="vec">**v**</span> is always "−1 of <span class="ihat">**î**</span> plus 2 of <span class="jhat">**ĵ**</span>", before *and* after the move.

## Numbers

The matrix's columns are where <span class="ihat">**î**</span> and <span class="jhat">**ĵ**</span> land: <Formula tex="(1, -2)" /> and <Formula tex="(3, 0)" />.
Before the move, <Formula tex="\vec{v} = (-1, 2)" /> means "−1 of î plus 2 of ĵ". So after the move, it is −1 of the first column plus 2 of the second:

<Formula display tex="\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 3 \\ -2 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} = -1 \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -2 \end{bmatrix} + 2 \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} 6 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 5 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}" />

## Explain

Every vector is some amount of <span class="ihat">**î**</span> plus some amount of <span class="jhat">**ĵ**</span>: <Formula tex="(x, y) = x\,\hat{\imath} + y\,\hat{\jmath}" />.
A linear transformation keeps sums and stretches (lesson 2.1), so

<Formula display tex="T(x\,\hat{\imath} + y\,\hat{\jmath}) = x\,T(\hat{\imath}) + y\,T(\hat{\jmath})" />

So **two arrows decide everything**: once you know where î and ĵ land, you know where every point lands. A matrix is just those two landing spots, written side by side as columns:

<Formula display tex="\begin{bmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \end{bmatrix} = x \begin{bmatrix} a \\ c \end{bmatrix} + y \begin{bmatrix} b \\ d \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} ax + by \\ cx + dy \end{bmatrix}" />

That is matrix-times-vector: **x copies of the first column plus y copies of the second.** The grid lines stay parallel and evenly spaced because every point follows the same recipe.

## Predict

With the same matrix, where does <Formula tex="(1, 1)" /> land? Work it out first. Then press **Rewind**, drag <span class="vec">**v**</span> to <Formula tex="(1, 1)" />, and press **Play** to check.

<details>
  <summary>Reveal</summary>
  One of each column: <Formula tex="(1, -2) + (3, 0) = (4, -2)" />.
</details>

## Go deeper

Textbooks often teach the "row" way first: the top number is row 1 dotted with <Formula tex="(x, y)" />, so <Formula tex="1 \cdot (-1) + 3 \cdot 2 = 5" />. It gives the same answer, and the dot product from lesson 1.5 is the reason it works. MIT 18.06 Lecture 1 (linked below) calls these the *row picture* and the *column picture* and shows both.
