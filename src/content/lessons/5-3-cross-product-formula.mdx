## Question

Lesson 5.2 gave a formula for <Formula tex="\vec{v} \times \vec{w}" /> and a picture (right angles, length = area). Why does **that** formula give **that** picture?

## Play

The violet arrow <span class="space">**(x, y, z)**</span> and the parallelogram of <span class="vec">**v**</span> and <span class="vec2">**w**</span> make a slanted box. Move <span class="space">**(x, y, z)**</span> and compare the two lines at the bottom: **base area × height**, and **(x, y, z) · (v × w)**. They always match.

Try moving <span class="space">**(x, y, z)**</span> sideways (only x or y). The height doesn't change, so the volume doesn't either.

## Numbers

With <Formula tex="\vec{v} = (2, 0, 0)" />, <Formula tex="\vec{w} = (1, 2, 0)" /> and <Formula tex="(x, y, z) = (1, 1, 2)" />:

<Formula display tex="\vec{v} \times \vec{w} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \cdot 0 - 0 \cdot 2 \\ 0 \cdot 1 - 2 \cdot 0 \\ 2 \cdot 2 - 0 \cdot 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix}" />

The base (the parallelogram on the floor) has area 4. The box's height is how far <Formula tex="(1, 1, 2)" /> rises off the floor: 2. So:

<Formula display tex="\text{volume} = 4 \times 2 = 8, \qquad (1, 1, 2) \cdot (0, 0, 4) = 0 + 0 + 8 = 8" />

It is also the determinant of the three columns (Lesson 3.3's volume): <Formula tex="\det \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 & 2 \\ 2 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix} = 8" />.

## Explain

**Step 1: a volume machine.** Keep <Formula tex="\vec{v}" /> and <Formula tex="\vec{w}" /> fixed. Feed in any <Formula tex="(x, y, z)" /> and get back the signed volume of the box it makes with them:

<Formula display tex="f(x, y, z) = \det \begin{bmatrix} x & v_1 & w_1 \\ y & v_2 & w_2 \\ z & v_3 & w_3 \end{bmatrix} = x\,(v_2 w_3 - v_3 w_2) + y\,(v_3 w_1 - v_1 w_3) + z\,(v_1 w_2 - v_2 w_1)" />

**Step 2: it's linear, so duality applies.** The answer is <Formula tex="x" />, <Formula tex="y" /> and <Formula tex="z" />, each times a fixed number, added up. So <Formula tex="f" /> is a 1×3 matrix, and by duality (Lesson 5.1) it is a dot product with **some** vector <Formula tex="\vec{p}" />:

<Formula display tex="f(x, y, z) = \vec{p} \cdot (x, y, z), \qquad \vec{p} = \begin{bmatrix} v_2 w_3 - v_3 w_2 \\ v_3 w_1 - v_1 w_3 \\ v_1 w_2 - v_2 w_1 \end{bmatrix}" />

That <Formula tex="\vec{p}" /> is exactly the cross product formula.

**Step 3: what must p look like?** Measure the volume the other way, as **base × height**. The base is the parallelogram of <Formula tex="\vec{v}" /> and <Formula tex="\vec{w}" />. The height is how far <Formula tex="(x, y, z)" /> reaches **straight out** of that base: drop it onto the line at right angles to the base (Lesson 5.1's dashed line, in 3D). So

<Formula display tex="\text{volume} = (\text{base area}) \times \big((x, y, z) \text{ dropped onto the line at right angles to } \vec{v}, \vec{w}\big)" />

By duality, "drop onto that line, then multiply by the base area" is a dot product with the vector that **points along that line** and is **as long as the base area**.

**Both ways give the same volume for every (x, y, z), so the two vectors are the same.** <Formula tex="\vec{p} = \vec{v} \times \vec{w}" /> is at right angles to <Formula tex="\vec{v}" /> and <Formula tex="\vec{w}" />, and its length is the parallelogram's area. The right-hand rule is the sign of the determinant: a box built the right-handed way has positive volume.

## Predict

Without any working: what is <Formula tex="\vec{v} \cdot (\vec{v} \times \vec{w})" />?

<details>
  <summary>Reveal</summary>
  0. It's the volume of the box made by <Formula tex="\vec{v}" />, <Formula tex="\vec{v}" /> and <Formula tex="\vec{w}" />. Two sides are the same arrow, so the box is flat: no volume. That's another way to see that <Formula tex="\vec{v} \times \vec{w}" /> is at right angles to <Formula tex="\vec{v}" />. Set <span class="space">**(x, y, z)**</span> equal to <span class="vec">**v**</span> in the demo to check.

## Go deeper

**The triple product.** <Formula tex="\vec{a} \cdot (\vec{b} \times \vec{c})" /> is called the **scalar triple product**. It equals <Formula tex="\det[\vec{a}\;\vec{b}\;\vec{c}]" />, so you can move the letters round in a circle without changing it: <Formula tex="\vec{a} \cdot (\vec{b} \times \vec{c}) = \vec{b} \cdot (\vec{c} \times \vec{a})" />.

**Why the î ĵ k̂ trick works.** Expanding the "determinant" with <Formula tex="\hat{\imath}, \hat{\jmath}, \hat{k}" /> in the first column is Step 1 with <Formula tex="x, y, z" /> replaced by <Formula tex="\hat{\imath}, \hat{\jmath}, \hat{k}" />. It writes out the numbers of <Formula tex="\vec{p}" /> with the right labels.

**This is 3Blue1Brown's favourite kind of proof**: find a linear function, use duality to turn it into a vector, then find that vector a second way, from the picture.
