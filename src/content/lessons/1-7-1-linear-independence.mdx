## Question

## Numbers

**Three vectors in 3D.** Are <Formula tex="\mathbf{v}_1 = (1, 2, 0)" />, <Formula tex="\mathbf{v}_2 = (0, 1, 1)" /> and <Formula tex="\mathbf{v}_3 = (2, 5, 1)" /> independent? Ask whether <Formula tex="x_1\mathbf{v}_1 + x_2\mathbf{v}_2 + x_3\mathbf{v}_3 = \mathbf{0}" /> has a solution other than all 0s. Row reduce the matrix with these columns:

<Formula display tex="\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 2 \\ 2 & 1 & 5 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \xrightarrow{R_2 \to R_2 - 2R_1} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 2 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \xrightarrow{R_3 \to R_3 - R_2} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 2 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}" />

Column 3 has no pivot, so <Formula tex="x_3" /> is free: there **is** a nontrivial solution, and the vectors are **dependent**. Take <Formula tex="x_3 = 1" />: then <Formula tex="x_2 = -1" /> and <Formula tex="x_1 = -2" />, so

<Formula display tex="-2\mathbf{v}_1 - \mathbf{v}_2 + \mathbf{v}_3 = \mathbf{0}, \qquad\text{that is}\qquad \mathbf{v}_3 = 2\mathbf{v}_1 + \mathbf{v}_2." />

Check: <Formula tex="2(1, 2, 0) + (0, 1, 1) = (2, 5, 1)" />. ✓ <Formula tex="\mathbf{v}_3" /> adds nothing new: the three vectors only span a plane.

## Explain

**The definition.** Vectors <Formula tex="\mathbf{v}_1, \dots, \mathbf{v}_p" /> are *linearly independent* when

<Formula display tex="x_1\mathbf{v}_1 + x_2\mathbf{v}_2 + \cdots + x_p\mathbf{v}_p = \mathbf{0}" />

has **only the trivial solution**, all <Formula tex="x_i = 0" />. If some other choice of weights also gives <Formula tex="\mathbf{0}" />, they are *linearly dependent*, and that choice is a *linear dependence relation*, like <Formula tex="-2\mathbf{v}_1 - \mathbf{v}_2 + \mathbf{v}_3 = \mathbf{0}" /> above.

**How to test it.** Put the vectors in the columns of a matrix <Formula tex="A" />. The equation above is just <Formula tex="A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}" /> (Lesson 1.4.1), so by Lesson 1.5.1: **the columns of A are independent exactly when every column of A has a pivot** (no free unknowns).

**What dependent means.** A set of two or more vectors is dependent exactly when **at least one of them is a mix of the others**. In the picture: two vectors are dependent when they lie on one line through the origin, and their span is only that line instead of the whole plane.

**Three quick tests**, no row reduction needed:

- **Two vectors** are dependent exactly when one is a multiple of the other.
- **Too many vectors**: more vectors than entries in each (like 4 vectors in <Formula tex="\mathbb{R}^3" />) are always dependent. A <Formula tex="3 \times 4" /> matrix has at most 3 pivots, so one column is left without.
- **The zero vector**: any set that contains <Formula tex="\mathbf{0}" /> is dependent, because <Formula tex="1 \cdot \mathbf{0} = \mathbf{0}" /> is already a nontrivial relation.

If none of them applies, row reduce.

## Predict

<Formula tex="\mathbf{v} = (1, 0)" /> and <Formula tex="\mathbf{w} = (0, 0)" />. Independent or dependent?

## Go deeper

**Not "pairwise".** Three vectors can be dependent even when no two of them are multiples of each other, like the example in Numbers. Dependence is about the whole set.

**Where this goes.** A set of vectors that is independent **and** spans a space is called a *basis* of it. The book introduces that in §2.8 and §4.3. MIT 18.06 Lecture 9 (linked below) covers independence, basis and dimension together.
