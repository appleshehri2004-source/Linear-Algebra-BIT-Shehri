## Question

So far you've started with a vector <Formula tex="\vec{x}" /> and asked where the matrix <Formula tex="A" /> sends it. Now turn it round: you know where something **landed**, at <Formula tex="\vec{v}" />. Where did it **start**?

## Play

Drag the tip of the yellow <span class="vec">**x**</span>. The gold arrow is <span class="sum">**Ax**</span>, where <Formula tex="A" /> sends <span class="vec">**x**</span>, and it moves as you drag. Get <span class="sum">**Ax**</span> onto the target <Formula tex="\vec{v}" />.

<SolveGame client:visible matrix={[[2, 1], [-1, 1]]} />

The faint green and red arrows are the columns of <Formula tex="A" />: where <span class="ihat">**î**</span> and <span class="jhat">**ĵ**</span> land.

## Numbers

Take <Formula tex="\vec{v} = (1, 5)" />. The answer is <Formula tex="\vec{x} = (2, 3)" />: 2 copies of column 1 plus 3 copies of column 2.

<Formula display tex="\begin{bmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix} = 2 \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} + 3 \begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 5 \end{bmatrix}" />

Write <Formula tex="\vec{x} = (x, y)" /> and read the two rows of that sum one at a time. You get two ordinary equations:

<Formula display tex="\begin{cases} 2x - y = 1 \\ x + y = 5 \end{cases}" />

That is a **system of equations**, and it's the same question as "which <Formula tex="\vec{x}" /> does <Formula tex="A" /> send to <Formula tex="\vec{v}" />?"

## Explain

<Formula display tex="A\vec{x} = \vec{v} \quad\text{means}\quad \text{which } \vec{x} \text{ lands on } \vec{v}\text{?}" />

There are two ways to picture the same system.

- **Row picture.** Each equation is a line in the plane. The answer is the point on **both** lines: where they cross.
- **Column picture.** The answer says how much of each column to use so that, added tip to tail, they reach <Formula tex="\vec{v}" />. That's the game you just played.

<RowColumnPicture client:visible matrix={[[2, 1], [-1, 1]]} target={[1, 5]} />

Both pictures give <Formula tex="(2, 3)" />. The row picture is how equations are usually taught at school. The column picture is the one that connects to everything in this course: solving a system is **undoing a transformation**.

When <Formula tex="\det A \ne 0" />, <Formula tex="A" /> doesn't squash the plane, so every <Formula tex="\vec{v}" /> comes from exactly **one** <Formula tex="\vec{x}" />. The lines cross at exactly one point.

## Predict

Same <Formula tex="A" />. Which <Formula tex="\vec{x}" /> lands on <Formula tex="\vec{v} = (-4, 1)" />? Try the two equations <Formula tex="2x - y = -4" /> and <Formula tex="x + y = 1" /> first. Then check in the game: drag <span class="vec">**x**</span> to your answer and read <span class="sum">**Ax**</span>.

<details>
  <summary>Reveal</summary>
  Add the two equations: <Formula tex="3x = -3" />, so <Formula tex="x = -1" />, and then <Formula tex="y = 1 - x = 2" />. Check: <Formula tex="-1 \cdot (2, 1) + 2 \cdot (-1, 1) = (-4, 1)" />.

## Go deeper

What if <Formula tex="\det A = 0" />? Then <Formula tex="A" /> squashes the whole plane onto a line (Lesson 3.1). A target <Formula tex="\vec{v}" /> off that line is never hit: **no solution**, and in the row picture the two lines are parallel. A target on the line is hit by a whole line of different <Formula tex="\vec{x}" />s: **infinitely many solutions**, and the two equations are the same line. Lesson 4.3 shows why this is exactly when <Formula tex="A" /> can't be undone.
