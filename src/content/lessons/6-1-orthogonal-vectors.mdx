## Question

How can you tell that two arrows meet at a right angle **without a protractor**, just from their numbers? And can a whole line or plane be at right angles to another one?

## Numbers

<Formula tex="\vec{v} = (3, 1)" /> and <Formula tex="\vec{w} = (-1, 3)" />:

<Formula display tex="\vec{v} \cdot \vec{w} = 3 \cdot (-1) + 1 \cdot 3 = -3 + 3 = 0" />

So they are perpendicular. The same works in 3D: <Formula tex="(1, 2, 2) \cdot (2, 1, -2) = 2 + 2 - 4 = 0" />.

The matrix with rows <Formula tex="(1, 2)" /> and <Formula tex="(2, 4)" /> squashes <Formula tex="(-2, 1)" /> to 0. Check it against the first row: <Formula tex="(1, 2) \cdot (-2, 1) = -2 + 2 = 0" />. The null space is perpendicular to the row.

## Explain

**Zero dot product means a right angle.** From Lesson 1.5, <Formula tex="\vec{v} \cdot \vec{w} = |\vec{v}|\,|\vec{w}| \cos\theta" />. If neither arrow is 0, the only way to get 0 is <Formula tex="\cos\theta = 0" />, which means <Formula tex="\theta = 90^\circ" />. "Orthogonal" is the grown-up word for perpendicular.

**Pythagoras is hiding here.** For perpendicular arrows, <Formula tex="|\vec{v} + \vec{w}|^2 = |\vec{v}|^2 + |\vec{w}|^2" />. The cross terms <Formula tex="2\,\vec{v} \cdot \vec{w}" /> are 0.

**Whole spaces can be orthogonal.** Two subspaces are orthogonal when **every** arrow in one is perpendicular to **every** arrow in the other.

**Row space ⟂ null space.** <Formula tex="A\vec{x} = 0" /> says each row dotted with <Formula tex="\vec{x}" /> is 0. So <Formula tex="\vec{x}" /> is perpendicular to every row, and so to every mix of rows: the whole row space. In the same way, the column space is perpendicular to the left null space.

## Predict

Which number <Formula tex="k" /> makes <Formula tex="(2, 3)" /> perpendicular to <Formula tex="(k, 2)" />?

## Go deeper

**Complements.** In 3D, the null space of the matrix with rows <Formula tex="(1, 1, 1)" /> is a whole plane. It is **all** the vectors perpendicular to the row. Dimensions add up: 1 + 2 = 3. That is why the row space and the null space are called orthogonal complements.

**Not every pair of perpendicular spaces is a complement.** In 3D, two perpendicular lines are orthogonal, but together they don't fill space. A complement is everything that is perpendicular.

**Why this module matters.** The next lessons all use one idea: the closest point is where the error is perpendicular. That gives projections (6.2), the best fit line (6.3) and clean right-angled bases (6.4).
