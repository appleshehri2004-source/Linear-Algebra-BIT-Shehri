## Question

We describe every arrow with two numbers: how many <span class="ihat">**î**</span> and how many <span class="jhat">**ĵ**</span>. But that choice is ours. Jennifer measures with her own two arrows, <span class="ihat">**b₁**</span> and <span class="jhat">**b₂**</span>. She looks at the **same** arrow and says different numbers. How do we translate?

## Play

The grey grid is ours. The blue grid is Jennifer's: it's made from her <span class="ihat">**b₁**</span> = (2, 1) and <span class="jhat">**b₂**</span> = (−1, 1), written in our numbers. Both grids share the origin.

Drag the <span class="vec">**yellow arrow**</span>. It has two labels: **ours** (how many î and ĵ) and **hers** (how many b₁ and b₂). Switch to **Hers** and the arrow snaps to the corners of her grid, where her numbers are whole.

Then drag <span class="ihat">**b₁**</span> and <span class="jhat">**b₂**</span> to give Jennifer any grid you like. Try putting them on one line.

## Numbers

Jennifer says <Formula tex="\begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}" />. She means "−1 copies of b₁ plus 2 copies of b₂". Work it out in our numbers:

<Formula display tex="-1 \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} + 2 \begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -4 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} \quad\text{that is}\quad \begin{bmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -4 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}" />

The other way: our <Formula tex="(3, 2)" /> in her numbers. Undo <Formula tex="P" /> with its inverse (Lesson 2.2.1):

<Formula display tex="P^{-1} = \frac{1}{3} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ -1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad P^{-1} \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} = \frac{1}{3} \begin{bmatrix} 3 + 2 \\ -3 + 4 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 5/3 \\ 1/3 \end{bmatrix}" />

Check: <Formula tex="\tfrac{5}{3}(2, 1) + \tfrac{1}{3}(-1, 1) = (\tfrac{10}{3} - \tfrac{1}{3}, \; \tfrac{5}{3} + \tfrac{1}{3}) = (3, 2)" />. ✓

## Explain

**One arrow, two languages.** The yellow arrow never changes. Only the **numbers** used to describe it change, because Jennifer and we use different measuring sticks.

**P translates hers → ours.** Put her basis vectors, written in our numbers, as the columns of a matrix:

<Formula display tex="P = \begin{bmatrix} | & | \\ \vec{b}_1 & \vec{b}_2 \\ | & | \end{bmatrix}" />

Her numbers <Formula tex="(x, y)" /> mean <Formula tex="x\,\vec{b}_1 + y\,\vec{b}_2" />, and that is exactly <Formula tex="P \begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \end{bmatrix}" /> (Lesson 1.9.1: a matrix times a vector is that many copies of each column).

**P⁻¹ translates ours → hers.** To go back, undo <Formula tex="P" />.

**A strange-sounding fact.** As a transformation, <Formula tex="P" /> moves **our** grid onto **hers** (it sends î to b₁ and ĵ to b₂). But as a translator it turns **her** numbers into **ours**. Both are true: <Formula tex="P" /> takes her numbers, treats them as if they were ours, and moves that arrow to where she really means.

**Not a basis.** If b₁ and b₂ lie on one line, her grid squashes flat. Most arrows can't be described at all, and <Formula tex="\det P = 0" />, so there is no <Formula tex="P^{-1}" /> (Lesson 2.2.1).

## Predict

In her numbers, an arrow is <Formula tex="(1, 1)" />. What is it in ours?

## Go deeper

**Our basis isn't special to space.** Space has no built-in grid. î and ĵ are just the arrows **we** happened to choose. To Jennifer, b₁ is <Formula tex="(1, 0)" /> and b₂ is <Formula tex="(0, 1)" />.

**Why bother with another basis?** Some problems are much simpler in the right grid. Eigenvectors (a later module) find a grid where a transformation only stretches, with no turning at all.

**Image compression** (the MIT lecture) is change of basis: a picture is rewritten in a basis of smooth wave patterns, and the small numbers are thrown away.
