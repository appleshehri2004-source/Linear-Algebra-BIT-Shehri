## Question

## Numbers

In 3D there are three basis arrows: <span class="ihat">**î**</span> <Formula tex="= (1, 0, 0)" />, <span class="jhat">**ĵ**</span> <Formula tex="= (0, 1, 0)" /> and <span class="khat">**k̂**</span> <Formula tex="= (0, 0, 1)" />. A 3D transformation is decided by where those three land, so its matrix has three columns of three numbers.

The **turn about y** sends <span class="ihat">**î**</span> to <Formula tex="(0, 0, -1)" />, keeps <span class="jhat">**ĵ**</span> where it is, and sends <span class="khat">**k̂**</span> to <Formula tex="(1, 0, 0)" />:

<Formula display tex="\begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ -1 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 3 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = 2 \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix} + 3 \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} + 1 \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 3 \\ -2 \end{bmatrix}" />

The **shear** keeps <span class="ihat">**î**</span> and <span class="jhat">**ĵ**</span> and tips <span class="khat">**k̂**</span> over to <Formula tex="(1, 0, 1)" />. The **scale** makes everything twice as long in x and half as tall in z.

## Explain

Nothing new happens in 3D. It is the same idea as in 2D, with one more column:

- **The columns are where î, ĵ and k̂ land.** To multiply, take <Formula tex="x" /> copies of column 1, <Formula tex="y" /> of column 2 and <Formula tex="z" /> of column 3, and add.
- **Grid lines stay straight, evenly spaced, and the origin stays put.** So a cube becomes a slanted box (a *parallelepiped*), never a curved one.
- **The volume line under the editor is the 3D determinant.** The turn and the shear keep the volume at 1. The scale gives <Formula tex="2 \times 1 \times 0.5 = 1" /> as well! Try changing the 0.5 to 2: the box gets 4 times bigger.
- **Composition works the same way.** Doing <Formula tex="B" /> then <Formula tex="A" /> is the product <Formula tex="AB" />, and the order still matters.

## Predict

Turn about y twice in a row. Where does <span class="ihat">**î**</span> end up?

<details>
  <summary>Reveal</summary>
  The first turn sends <Formula tex="(1, 0, 0)" /> to <Formula tex="(0, 0, -1)" />. The second turn sends that to <Formula tex="-1 \cdot (1, 0, 0) = (-1, 0, 0)" />. Two quarter turns make a half turn, so <span class="ihat">**î**</span> points the opposite way. Type the matrix with columns <Formula tex="(-1, 0, 0)" />, <Formula tex="(0, 1, 0)" />, <Formula tex="(0, 0, -1)" /> into the editor to check.

## Go deeper

Try a matrix whose third column is the sum of the first two, for example columns <Formula tex="(1, 0, 0)" />, <Formula tex="(0, 1, 0)" />, <Formula tex="(1, 1, 0)" />. The cube gets squashed flat, the volume line says 0, and all of space lands on one plane. Just like in 2D, a determinant of 0 means the matrix can't be undone.

The 3D determinant is the volume of the box made by the three columns: <Formula tex="\det A = \hat{\imath}' \cdot (\hat{\jmath}' \times \hat{k}')" />, where the primes mean "where it landed" and <Formula tex="\times" /> is the *cross product*. You'll meet both again in Module 3.
