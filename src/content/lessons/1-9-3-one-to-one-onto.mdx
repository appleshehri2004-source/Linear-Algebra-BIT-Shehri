## Explain

**Onto.** <Formula tex="T : \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^m" /> maps <Formula tex="\mathbb{R}^n" /> *onto* <Formula tex="\mathbb{R}^m" /> when every <Formula tex="\mathbf{b}" /> in <Formula tex="\mathbb{R}^m" /> is the image of **at least one** <Formula tex="\mathbf{x}" />. In other words, the range is the whole codomain: <Formula tex="T(\mathbf{x}) = \mathbf{b}" /> always has a solution.

**One-to-one.** <Formula tex="T" /> is *one-to-one* when every <Formula tex="\mathbf{b}" /> is the image of **at most one** <Formula tex="\mathbf{x}" />: two different inputs never land on the same output. It may still miss some points.

**One test for one-to-one.** For a linear <Formula tex="T" />, it is enough to look at <Formula tex="\mathbf{0}" />: **T is one-to-one exactly when <Formula tex="T(\mathbf{x}) = \mathbf{0}" /> has only the trivial solution.** Why? If <Formula tex="T(\mathbf{u}) = T(\mathbf{v})" /> with <Formula tex="\mathbf{u} \ne \mathbf{v}" />, then <Formula tex="T(\mathbf{u} - \mathbf{v}) = \mathbf{0}" /> with <Formula tex="\mathbf{u} - \mathbf{v} \ne \mathbf{0}" />. And the other way round, a nontrivial <Formula tex="\mathbf{x}" /> with <Formula tex="T(\mathbf{x}) = \mathbf{0}" /> lands on the same spot as <Formula tex="\mathbf{0}" /> does.

**Both, from the standard matrix A** (everything from §1.4 and §1.7 comes together here):

- <Formula tex="T" /> maps onto <Formula tex="\mathbb{R}^m" /> ⇔ the columns of <Formula tex="A" /> span <Formula tex="\mathbb{R}^m" /> ⇔ **a pivot in every row**.
- <Formula tex="T" /> is one-to-one ⇔ the columns of <Formula tex="A" /> are linearly independent ⇔ **a pivot in every column**.

**What the size already tells you.** An <Formula tex="m \times n" /> matrix has at most one pivot per row and one per column.

- More columns than rows (<Formula tex="n > m" />): some column has no pivot, so **never one-to-one**.
- More rows than columns (<Formula tex="m > n" />): some row has no pivot, so **never onto**.
- Square: both are possible, and it can still be neither, like **Project**.

## Predict

Can a linear transformation <Formula tex="T : \mathbb{R}^3 \to \mathbb{R}^2" /> be one-to-one?

<details>
  <summary>Reveal</summary>
  No. Its standard matrix is <Formula tex="2 \times 3" />: at most 2 pivots for 3 columns, so one column has no pivot and <Formula tex="T(\mathbf{x}) = \mathbf{0}" /> has a nontrivial solution. Squashing 3D space into a plane always piles some points on top of each other.
</details>

## Go deeper

**Square matrices are special.** For an <Formula tex="n \times n" /> matrix, "a pivot in every column" and "a pivot in every row" are the same thing (<Formula tex="n" /> pivots), so one-to-one and onto come together or not at all. That is the start of the *Invertible Matrix Theorem* in §2.3.

**Undoing a transformation.** A one-to-one and onto <Formula tex="T" /> can be run backwards: each output came from exactly one input. That backwards map is the *inverse*, the subject of §2.2.
