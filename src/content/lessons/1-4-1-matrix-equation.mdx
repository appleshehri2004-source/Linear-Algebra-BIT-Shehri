## Question

In Lesson 1.3.3, "is <Formula tex="\mathbf{b}" /> in the span?" meant finding weights with <Formula tex="x_1\mathbf{a}_1 + x_2\mathbf{a}_2 = \mathbf{b}" />. That is a lot to write. Can one short product say the same thing?

## Numbers

<Formula tex="A" /> has columns <Formula tex="(2, 1)" /> and <Formula tex="(-1, 1)" />. For <Formula tex="\mathbf{x} = (2, 3)" />, the product <Formula tex="A\mathbf{x}" /> is **2 of column 1 plus 3 of column 2**:

<Formula display tex="\begin{bmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix} = 2 \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} + 3 \begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 5 \end{bmatrix}" />

**The quick way, one row at a time.** Each entry of <Formula tex="A\mathbf{x}" /> is a row of <Formula tex="A" /> times <Formula tex="\mathbf{x}" />, number by number, added up. The top entry is <Formula tex="2 \cdot 2 + (-1) \cdot 3 = 1" />, the bottom one <Formula tex="1 \cdot 2 + 1 \cdot 3 = 5" />. Same answer.

It works for any size, as long as <Formula tex="\mathbf{x}" /> has one number per column of <Formula tex="A" />:

<Formula display tex="\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 2 \\ 3 & -1 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 4 \\ 1 \\ -2 \end{bmatrix} = 4 \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix} + 1 \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix} - 2 \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 9 \end{bmatrix}" />

A <Formula tex="2 \times 3" /> matrix times a vector with 2 numbers is **not defined**: there aren't enough weights for the columns.

## Explain

**The definition.** If <Formula tex="A" /> has columns <Formula tex="\mathbf{a}_1, \dots, \mathbf{a}_n" /> and <Formula tex="\mathbf{x}" /> has entries <Formula tex="x_1, \dots, x_n" />, then

<Formula display tex="A\mathbf{x} = x_1\mathbf{a}_1 + x_2\mathbf{a}_2 + \cdots + x_n\mathbf{a}_n." />

**One question, three ways to write it.** The same problem can be written as

- a **matrix equation**, <Formula tex="A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}" />,
- a **vector equation**, <Formula tex="x_1\mathbf{a}_1 + \cdots + x_n\mathbf{a}_n = \mathbf{b}" />,
- a **system**, with augmented matrix <Formula tex="[\,\mathbf{a}_1 \;\cdots\; \mathbf{a}_n \mid \mathbf{b}\,]" />.

All three have exactly the same solutions, so you pick the one that helps: the matrix equation is short to write, the vector equation gives the picture, and the augmented matrix is what you row reduce. Below, the same system drawn both ways: as two lines that cross (Lesson 1.1.1), and as columns that add up to <Formula tex="\mathbf{b}" />.

<RowColumnPicture client:visible matrix={[[2, 1], [-1, 1]]} target={[1, 5]} />

So <Formula tex="A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}" /> has a solution **exactly when <Formula tex="\mathbf{b}" /> is a mix of the columns of <Formula tex="A" />**.

**When can every b be reached?** For an <Formula tex="m \times n" /> matrix <Formula tex="A" />, these four sentences are either all true or all false:

1. <Formula tex="A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}" /> has a solution for **every** <Formula tex="\mathbf{b}" /> in <Formula tex="\mathbb{R}^m" />.
2. Every <Formula tex="\mathbf{b}" /> in <Formula tex="\mathbb{R}^m" /> is a mix of the columns of <Formula tex="A" />.
3. The columns of <Formula tex="A" /> span <Formula tex="\mathbb{R}^m" />.
4. <Formula tex="A" /> has a pivot in **every row**.

Number 4 is the one you can check: row reduce <Formula tex="A" /> (the coefficient matrix, not the augmented one) and count. If a row of an echelon form is all 0s, some <Formula tex="\mathbf{b}" /> will give that row <Formula tex="0 = c" /> with <Formula tex="c \ne 0" />.

**Two rules that make Ax easy to work with.** For any vectors <Formula tex="\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v}" /> and number <Formula tex="c" />:

<Formula display tex="A(\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v}) = A\mathbf{u} + A\mathbf{v} \qquad\text{and}\qquad A(c\,\mathbf{u}) = c\,(A\mathbf{u})" />

Both come straight from the definition: mixing the columns with <Formula tex="u_i + v_i" /> is the same as mixing with <Formula tex="u_i" />, mixing with <Formula tex="v_i" />, and adding. These two rules are the heart of §1.5 and §1.8.

## Predict

<Formula tex="A" /> is a <Formula tex="3 \times 2" /> matrix. Can <Formula tex="A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}" /> have a solution for every <Formula tex="\mathbf{b}" /> in <Formula tex="\mathbb{R}^3" />?

<details>
  <summary>Reveal</summary>
  No. A pivot needs its own column, so 2 columns give at most 2 pivots, and one of the 3 rows has none. In pictures: two arrows in 3D span at most a plane, and most of space is off it.
</details>
