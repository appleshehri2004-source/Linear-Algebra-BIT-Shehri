## Question

Most matrices have no eigenvectors at right angles, and some (rotations) have none at all. Is there *anything* simple that every matrix does?

## Numbers

For <Formula tex="A = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 0 \\ 4 & 5 \end{bmatrix}" /> (columns (3, 4) and (0, 5)), start with the symmetric matrix from the last lesson:

<Formula display tex="A^T A = \begin{bmatrix} 25 & 20 \\ 20 & 25 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \lambda = 45 \text{ along } (1, 1), \quad \lambda = 5 \text{ along } (1, -1)" />

The **singular values** are the square roots: <Formula tex="\sigma_1 = \sqrt{45} = 3\sqrt{5}" /> and <Formula tex="\sigma_2 = \sqrt{5}" />. The right singular vectors <Formula tex="\vec{v}_1, \vec{v}_2" /> are those eigenvectors made 1 long. Then <Formula tex="\vec{u}_1 = A\vec{v}_1 / \sigma_1" />:

<Formula display tex="A\vec{v}_1 = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt 2}\begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 9 \end{bmatrix}, \quad |A\vec{v}_1| = \tfrac{\sqrt{90}}{\sqrt 2} = 3\sqrt5 = \sigma_1 \;\checkmark" />

Check: <Formula tex="\sigma_1 \sigma_2 = 3\sqrt5 \cdot \sqrt5 = 15 = |\det A|" />.

## Explain

**The SVD.** Every matrix, with no exceptions, can be written as

<Formula display tex="A = U \Sigma V^T" />

where <Formula tex="U" /> and <Formula tex="V" /> are orthogonal (rotations, maybe with a flip) and <Formula tex="\Sigma" /> is diagonal with <Formula tex="\sigma_1 \geq \sigma_2 \geq 0" />. Read it right to left, like Lesson 5.3.1:

1. <Formula tex="V^T" /> turns the plane so that <Formula tex="\vec{v}_1, \vec{v}_2" /> lie on the axes. Lengths don't change.
2. <Formula tex="\Sigma" /> stretches along the axes, by <Formula tex="\sigma_1" /> and <Formula tex="\sigma_2" />. The circle becomes an ellipse.
3. <Formula tex="U" /> turns the ellipse into place, with its half-axes along <Formula tex="\vec{u}_1, \vec{u}_2" />.

That's why **Three steps** lands on exactly the same ellipse as **Direct**: they are the same matrix. The site's tests check that <Formula tex="U\Sigma V^T" /> matches A to 9 decimal places.

**Why it always works.** <Formula tex="A^T A" /> is symmetric, so it has perpendicular eigenvectors <Formula tex="\vec{v}_1, \vec{v}_2" />. Then <Formula tex="A\vec{v}_1" /> and <Formula tex="A\vec{v}_2" /> are perpendicular too: <Formula tex="(A\vec{v}_1)\cdot(A\vec{v}_2) = \vec{v}_1^T A^T A \vec{v}_2 = \lambda_2\, \vec{v}_1 \cdot \vec{v}_2 = 0" />. So two arrows at right angles go in, and two arrows at right angles come out. Those are the ellipse's axes.

**Compared with eigenvectors.** An eigenvector stays on its own line: <Formula tex="A\vec{v} = \lambda\vec{v}" />. A singular vector may move to a different line, but its right angle with the other one survives: <Formula tex="A\vec{v}_i = \sigma_i \vec{u}_i" />. For a symmetric matrix with λs above 0 the two ideas agree.

**What the σs tell you.**

- <Formula tex="\sigma_1" /> is the biggest stretch of any arrow 1 long, and <Formula tex="\sigma_2" /> the smallest.
- <Formula tex="\sigma_1\sigma_2 = |\det A|" />, since the circle's area <Formula tex="\pi" /> becomes the ellipse's area <Formula tex="\pi\sigma_1\sigma_2" />.
- A rotation has <Formula tex="\sigma_1 = \sigma_2 = 1" />. A rank-1 matrix has <Formula tex="\sigma_2 = 0" />: the ellipse is flattened to a segment. The rank is the number of σs that aren't 0.

## Predict

<Formula tex="A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 0 & -3 \end{bmatrix}" />. What are <Formula tex="\sigma_1" /> and <Formula tex="\sigma_2" />? Type it into the view to check.

<details>
  <summary>Reveal</summary>
  <Formula tex="\sigma_1 = 3" /> and <Formula tex="\sigma_2 = 2" />. The circle is stretched 3 along y and 2 along x, and the minus sign only flips it. ## Go deeper

**Compressing images.** A photo is a big matrix of brightness numbers. Its SVD writes it as a sum <Formula tex="\sigma_1\vec{u}_1\vec{v}_1^T + \sigma_2\vec{u}_2\vec{v}_2^T + \cdots" />, biggest pieces first. Keep only the first 50 pieces of a 1000×1000 photo and it still looks almost the same, from a tenth of the numbers.

**Data science.** Principal component analysis (PCA) is the SVD of a table of data. <Formula tex="\vec{v}_1" /> is the direction in which the data spreads the most.

**Any shape, any size.** The SVD works for non-square matrices too (Lesson 1.8.2). A 3×2 matrix turns the unit circle into a flat ellipse inside 3D space. <Formula tex="U" /> is then 3×3 and <Formula tex="\Sigma" /> is 3×2.
