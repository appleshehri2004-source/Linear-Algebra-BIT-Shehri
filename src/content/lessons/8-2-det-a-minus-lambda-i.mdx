## Question

Sweeping a probe round the circle finds eigenvectors by luck. Is there a way to **calculate** the eigenvalues of <Formula tex="A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 2 \\ 1 & 3 \end{bmatrix}" />?

## Play

Turn the λ knob. The plane shows what <Formula tex="A - \lambda I" /> does to it, and underneath is the live value of <Formula tex="\det(A - \lambda I)" />.

Start at λ = 0 and turn slowly to the right. Watch for the moment the plane **squishes onto a line**. (The slider clicks into place there.) Is there a second one?

<LambdaKnob client:visible matrix={[[2, 1], [2, 3]]} start={0} min={-1} max={6} />

## Numbers

Take λ off both diagonal numbers, then work out the determinant:

<Formula display tex="\det(A - \lambda I) = \det \begin{bmatrix} 2 - \lambda & 2 \\ 1 & 3 - \lambda \end{bmatrix} = (2 - \lambda)(3 - \lambda) - 2 \cdot 1 = \lambda^2 - 5\lambda + 4" />

That is 0 when <Formula tex="(\lambda - 1)(\lambda - 4) = 0" />: at **λ = 1** and **λ = 4**, exactly where the plane squished.

At λ = 1, <Formula tex="A - I = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}" /> squashes <Formula tex="(-2, 1)" /> to 0. So <Formula tex="A(-2, 1) = 1 \cdot (-2, 1)" />: check it, <Formula tex="(2 \cdot (-2) + 2,\; -2 + 3) = (-2, 1)" /> ✓

## Explain

**Rewrite the eigen-equation.** <Formula tex="A\vec{v} = \lambda\vec{v}" /> is the same as <Formula tex="A\vec{v} - \lambda\vec{v} = \vec{0}" />, and <Formula tex="\lambda\vec{v} = \lambda I \vec{v}" />, so

<Formula display tex="(A - \lambda I)\,\vec{v} = \vec{0}." />

**A non-zero arrow squashed to 0.** We need a **non-zero** <Formula tex="\vec{v}" /> that <Formula tex="A - \lambda I" /> sends to the origin. Only a matrix that squishes the plane flat can do that (Module 3: then something non-zero lands on 0, the null space of Lesson 4.5). Squishing flat means

<Formula display tex="\det(A - \lambda I) = 0." />

**The knob in one sentence.** For most λ, <Formula tex="A - \lambda I" /> keeps the plane a plane: only 0 goes to 0, and there is no eigenvector. At an eigenvalue, the plane collapses to a line, and the cyan line of arrows that went to 0 are the eigenvectors.

**The characteristic polynomial.** <Formula tex="\det(A - \lambda I)" /> is always <Formula tex="\lambda^2 - (\text{trace})\lambda + \det A" /> for a 2×2 matrix. Its roots, the cyan dots on the little graph, are the eigenvalues.

Check the lines this lesson found with the probe from Lesson 8.1:

## Predict

For the rotation <Formula tex="\begin{bmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}" />, does the curve <Formula tex="\det(A - \lambda I)" /> ever touch 0?

<details>
  <summary>Reveal</summary>
  No. <Formula tex="\det \begin{bmatrix} -\lambda & -1 \\ 1 & -\lambda \end{bmatrix} = \lambda^2 + 1" />, which is at least 1 for every real λ. Its roots are λ = i and λ = −i, not real numbers. That matches the probe: no line ever lit up.
</details>

## Go deeper

**Finding the eigenvector after λ.** Once you know λ, solve <Formula tex="(A - \lambda I)\vec{v} = \vec{0}" /> with elimination (Lesson 4.2). Both rows are multiples of each other (that's what det = 0 means), so one equation is enough: for λ = 4, <Formula tex="-2x + 2y = 0" /> gives <Formula tex="\vec{v} = (1, 1)" />.

**3×3 matrices.** The same idea gives a cubic, <Formula tex="-\lambda^3 + \dots" />, with up to 3 roots. A cubic always has at least one real root, so every 3D transformation has at least one eigen-line (Lesson 8.1's Go deeper).

**Repeated roots.** The shear gives <Formula tex="(1 - \lambda)^2" />: one root, twice. It has only one eigen-line. 2I gives <Formula tex="(2 - \lambda)^2" /> too, but every line works. The polynomial alone can't tell those two apart.
