## Question

The sun is straight overhead and <span class="vec">**v**</span> casts a shadow on a line, or on the floor. Which point of the line or floor is **closest** to v? And what happens if you take the shadow of a shadow?

## Play

Drag <span class="vec">**v**</span> and the line's direction <span class="space">**a**</span>. The gold arrow is the shadow <Formula tex="P\vec{v}" />. The pink dashed arrow is the error <Formula tex="\vec{e} = \vec{v} - P\vec{v}" />. The small square marks the right angle. Try to make the square go away.

## Numbers

Project <Formula tex="\vec{v} = (1, 3)" /> onto the line through <Formula tex="\vec{a} = (2, 1)" />. (The demo starts with a = (4, 2): a longer arrow on the same line gives the same shadow.)

<Formula display tex="P\vec{v} = \frac{\vec{a} \cdot \vec{v}}{\vec{a} \cdot \vec{a}}\,\vec{a} = \frac{2 + 3}{4 + 1} \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \vec{e} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix} - \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}" />

Check the right angle: <Formula tex="\vec{e} \cdot \vec{a} = -2 + 2 = 0" />. ✓

The same thing as one matrix. For <Formula tex="\vec{a} = (1, 1)" />:

<Formula display tex="P = \frac{\vec{a}\,\vec{a}^T}{\vec{a}^T \vec{a}} = \frac{1}{2} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad P^2 = \frac{1}{4} \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 2 \\ 2 & 2 \end{bmatrix} = P" />

In 3D, <Formula tex="(1, 2, 3)" /> onto the floor spanned by <Formula tex="(1, 0, 0)" /> and <Formula tex="(0, 1, 0)" /> is <Formula tex="(1, 2, 0)" />. The error <Formula tex="(0, 0, 3)" /> points straight up.

## Explain

**Closest means perpendicular.** Slide a point along the line. Its distance to v is smallest exactly when the error meets the line at 90°. Any other point q on the line makes a right triangle with v and Pv, with the right angle at Pv. The distance from v to q is the hypotenuse, so it is longer than the error (Pythagoras).

**The formula comes from that right angle.** The shadow is some number <Formula tex="x" /> of copies of <Formula tex="\vec{a}" />. The error must be perpendicular to <Formula tex="\vec{a}" />:

<Formula display tex="\vec{a} \cdot (\vec{v} - x\,\vec{a}) = 0 \quad\Rightarrow\quad x = \frac{\vec{a} \cdot \vec{v}}{\vec{a} \cdot \vec{a}}" />

**A plane works the same way.** Put the two spanning arrows in the columns of <Formula tex="A" />. The error must be perpendicular to both columns: <Formula tex="A^T(\vec{v} - A\hat{x}) = 0" />. That gives <Formula tex="A^T A\,\hat{x} = A^T \vec{v}" />, the normal equations. You will see them again in Lesson 6.3.

**Projecting twice changes nothing.** After one projection the shadow is already on the line, so its own shadow is itself. In matrices, <Formula tex="P^2 = P" />. That is what you saw when you pressed Project again.

## Go deeper

**Projections squash.** A projection onto a line has determinant 0. It flattens the plane onto the line. Its null space is the line perpendicular to <Formula tex="\vec{a}" />: those arrows have no shadow at all.

**P is symmetric.** <Formula tex="\vec{a}\vec{a}^T" /> equals its own transpose, so <Formula tex="P^T = P" />. Together with <Formula tex="P^2 = P" />, this describes exactly the "straight overhead" projections.

**I − P is a projection too.** <Formula tex="(I - P)\vec{v} = \vec{e}" />, so it projects onto the perpendicular line instead. A vector splits into two perpendicular parts: <Formula tex="\vec{v} = P\vec{v} + (I - P)\vec{v}" />.
