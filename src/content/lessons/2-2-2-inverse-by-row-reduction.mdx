## Question

The 2×2 formula from Lesson 2.2.1 has no handy 3×3 or 4×4 version. **How do you find the inverse of a bigger matrix**, or find out that there isn't one?

## Numbers

The 2×2 example takes two steps:

<Formula display tex="\left[\begin{array}{cc|cc} 1 & 2 & 1 & 0 \\ 3 & 7 & 0 & 1 \end{array}\right] \xrightarrow{R_2 \to R_2 - 3R_1} \left[\begin{array}{cc|cc} 1 & 2 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & -3 & 1 \end{array}\right] \xrightarrow{R_1 \to R_1 - 2R_2} \left[\begin{array}{cc|cc} 1 & 0 & 7 & -2 \\ 0 & 1 & -3 & 1 \end{array}\right]" />

So <Formula tex="A^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} 7 & -2 \\ -3 & 1 \end{bmatrix}" />. The 2×2 formula agrees: here <Formula tex="ad - bc = 7 - 6 = 1" />.

Each of those row operations is itself a matrix. Doing <Formula tex="R_2 \to R_2 - 3R_1" /> to the identity gives

<Formula display tex="E = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ -3 & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad EA = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ -3 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 7 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}" />

Multiplying by <Formula tex="E" /> did the row operation.

## Explain

**Elementary matrices.** An **elementary matrix** is what you get by doing one row operation (a swap, a scaling, or adding a multiple of one row to another) to the identity. Then <Formula tex="EA" /> is <Formula tex="A" /> after that same row operation. Every elementary matrix is invertible: its inverse is the elementary matrix of the operation that undoes it. The inverse of the <Formula tex="E" /> above adds <Formula tex="3R_1" /> back:

<Formula display tex="E^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 3 & 1 \end{bmatrix}" />

**Why the algorithm works.** Suppose some row operations take <Formula tex="A" /> to <Formula tex="I" />. Write them as elementary matrices <Formula tex="E_1, \dots, E_k" />:

<Formula display tex="E_k \cdots E_2E_1A = I" />

So the product <Formula tex="E_k \cdots E_1" /> undoes <Formula tex="A" />: it **is** <Formula tex="A^{-1}" />. And <Formula tex="E_k \cdots E_1 = E_k \cdots E_1 I" /> is what you get by doing the same row operations to <Formula tex="I" />. Writing <Formula tex="A" /> and <Formula tex="I" /> side by side does both at once.

**The algorithm.** Row reduce <Formula tex="[\,A \;\; I\,]" />.

- If <Formula tex="A" /> is row equivalent to <Formula tex="I" />, you end with <Formula tex="[\,I \;\; A^{-1}\,]" />.
- If it isn't, a row of 0s appears in the left half, and <Formula tex="A" /> has **no inverse**.

So an <Formula tex="n \times n" /> matrix is invertible **exactly when it is row equivalent to** <Formula tex="I_n" />. The third matrix in the demo isn't: its row 3 is row 1 plus row 2, so elimination wipes row 3 out.

**Another view: one column at a time.** Column <Formula tex="j" /> of <Formula tex="A^{-1}" /> is <Formula tex="A^{-1}\mathbf{e}_j" />, the solution of <Formula tex="A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{e}_j" /> (where <Formula tex="\mathbf{e}_j" /> is column <Formula tex="j" /> of <Formula tex="I" />). Row reducing <Formula tex="[\,A \;\; I\,]" /> solves all <Formula tex="n" /> of these systems at once. If you only need one column, row reduce <Formula tex="[\,A \;\; \mathbf{e}_j\,]" />.

## Predict

Row reduce <Formula tex="[\,A \;\; I\,]" /> for the 3×3 matrix below. Will the left half reach <Formula tex="I" />? Decide by looking at the rows before you do any work.

<Formula display tex="A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -1 & 2 \\ 2 & 0 & 1 \\ 4 & -2 & 5 \end{bmatrix}" />

<details>
  <summary>Reveal</summary>
  No. Row 3 is 2 copies of row 1 plus row 2, so <Formula tex="R_3 \to R_3 - 2R_1 - R_2" /> turns it into a row of 0s. <Formula tex="A" /> isn't row equivalent to <Formula tex="I" />, so it has no inverse.
</details>

## Go deeper

**Gauss–Jordan.** MIT 18.06 Lecture 3 (linked below) calls this algorithm Gauss–Jordan elimination and runs it on the same block <Formula tex="[\,A \;\; I\,]" />.

**One side is enough.** The definition asks for both <Formula tex="CA = I" /> and <Formula tex="AC = I" />. For square matrices, one of them already forces the other. That's part of the Invertible Matrix Theorem in the next lesson.

**Undoing in reverse.** <Formula tex="A^{-1} = E_k \cdots E_1" />, so <Formula tex="A = E_1^{-1} \cdots E_k^{-1}" />: every invertible matrix is a product of elementary matrices. Any invertible transformation is a chain of swaps, stretches along one axis, and shears.
