## Question

Two equations with two unknowns, like <Formula tex="x + y = 3" /> and <Formula tex="x - y = 1" />. What does a solution look like? And can a system have **no** solution, or **lots** of them?

## Explain

**A system and its solutions.** A *linear equation* only adds up numbers times unknowns: no <Formula tex="x^2" />, no <Formula tex="xy" />. A *system* is a few of them at once. A *solution* is a choice of numbers that makes **every** equation true, and the *solution set* is all the solutions together.

**The picture.** With two unknowns, each equation is a straight line, and a solution is a point on both lines. Two lines can only do three things:

- **cross once**: exactly one solution,
- **be parallel**: no solution, because the lines never meet,
- **be the same line**: infinitely many solutions, every point on it.

They can never cross exactly twice, so a system never has exactly two solutions. With three unknowns each equation is a flat plane, and the same three outcomes are the only ones possible.

**Consistent or not.** A system with at least one solution is *consistent*. One with none is *inconsistent*.

**The augmented matrix** holds a system without the letters. Leave off the right-hand column and you have the *coefficient matrix*. A matrix with <Formula tex="m" /> rows and <Formula tex="n" /> columns is "<Formula tex="m \times n" />": rows first, like a seat number in a theatre.

**Three row moves**, the *elementary row operations*:

- **Replacement**: add a multiple of one row to another (<Formula tex="R_2 \to R_2 - 2R_1" />),
- **Interchange**: swap two rows (<Formula tex="R_2 \leftrightarrow R_3" />),
- **Scaling**: multiply a row by a number that isn't 0 (<Formula tex="R_3 \to -\tfrac17 R_3" />).

**Why the answer never changes.** If numbers solve equations 1 and 2, they also solve "equation 2 minus 2 times equation 1". And every move can be undone (add the 2 copies back, swap back, divide by the number again), so no new solutions sneak in either. That's what you saw in the picture: a move swaps one line for a different line **through the same crossing point**. Two matrices joined by row moves are called *row equivalent*, and their systems have the same solution set.

**How to spot no solution.** In **No solution**, the last row turns into <Formula tex="[\,0 \;\; 0 \mid -6\,]" />, which says <Formula tex="0 = -6" />. No numbers make that true, so the system is inconsistent. In **Infinitely many** it turns into <Formula tex="[\,0 \;\; 0 \mid 0\,]" />, which says <Formula tex="0 = 0" />: always true, so it tells you nothing new, and one line's worth of points is left.

So every system asks two questions. **Existence**: is there a solution at all? **Uniqueness**: if there is, is there only one?

## Predict

How many solutions do <Formula tex="x + 2y = 4" /> and <Formula tex="3x + 6y = 12" /> have?

## Go deeper

**The row picture and the column picture.** Everything here reads a system one equation (one *row*) at a time. You can also read it one *column* at a time, as a question about combining arrows. That is the next big idea, in §1.3 and §1.4. MIT 18.06 Lecture 1 (linked below) shows both pictures, with planes in 3D.

**Why computers love this.** The three moves are so simple that a computer can solve a thousand equations with a thousand unknowns in a fraction of a second. §1.2 turns them into a recipe that always works.
