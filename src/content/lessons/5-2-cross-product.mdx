## Question

Two arrows make a parallelogram. In 2D, one number can say how big it is and which way round it goes. In 3D the parallelogram can also be **tilted** in any direction. What single thing can hold its size **and** its tilt?

## Play

**First in 2D.** Drag <span class="vec">**v**</span> and <span class="vec2">**w**</span>. The number in the parallelogram is <Formula tex="\vec{v} \times \vec{w}" />, its **signed area**. Put <span class="vec">**v**</span> to the left of <span class="vec2">**w**</span> and it turns purple and negative. Then press **Swap v and w**.

**Now in 3D.** Move the sliders for <span class="vec">**v**</span> and <span class="vec2">**w**</span>. The <span class="cross">**teal arrow**</span> is <Formula tex="\vec{v} \times \vec{w}" />. Turn the view and check that it stands at right angles to the blue parallelogram. Then press **Swap v and w** and watch it flip.

## Numbers

**2D:** <Formula tex="\vec{v} \times \vec{w}" /> is the determinant with <Formula tex="\vec{v}" /> and <Formula tex="\vec{w}" /> as columns (Lesson 3.3). For <Formula tex="\vec{v} = (-3, 1)" /> and <Formula tex="\vec{w} = (2, 1)" />:

<Formula display tex="\vec{v} \times \vec{w} = \det \begin{bmatrix} -3 & 2 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = (-3)(1) - (2)(1) = -5" />

The area is 5, and the minus sign says <span class="vec">**v**</span> is on the left of <span class="vec2">**w**</span>.

**3D:** the answer is a vector:

<Formula display tex="\begin{bmatrix} v_1 \\ v_2 \\ v_3 \end{bmatrix} \times \begin{bmatrix} w_1 \\ w_2 \\ w_3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} v_2 w_3 - v_3 w_2 \\ v_3 w_1 - v_1 w_3 \\ v_1 w_2 - v_2 w_1 \end{bmatrix}" />

For <Formula tex="\vec{v} = (0, 0, 2)" /> and <Formula tex="\vec{w} = (0, 2, 0)" />:

<Formula display tex="\begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} \times \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 2 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \cdot 0 - 2 \cdot 2 \\ 2 \cdot 0 - 0 \cdot 0 \\ 0 \cdot 2 - 0 \cdot 0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -4 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}" />

Check: <Formula tex="\vec{v}" /> and <Formula tex="\vec{w}" /> make a 2 by 2 square, area 4, and <Formula tex="(-4, 0, 0)" /> is 4 long. It points along the x-axis, at right angles to that square in the yz-plane.

## Explain

In 3D, <Formula tex="\vec{v} \times \vec{w}" /> is the vector with these three properties:

1. **Length** = the area of the parallelogram made by <Formula tex="\vec{v}" /> and <Formula tex="\vec{w}" />.
2. **Direction**: at right angles to both <Formula tex="\vec{v}" /> and <Formula tex="\vec{w}" />, so it sticks straight out of the parallelogram.
3. **Which way out**: the **right-hand rule**. Point your right index finger along <span class="vec">**v**</span> and your middle finger along <span class="vec2">**w**</span>. Your thumb points along <Formula tex="\vec{v} \times \vec{w}" />.

Try the rule on the example: index finger up (<Formula tex="\vec{v}" /> is along z), middle finger along y. Your thumb points along **negative x**, just like <Formula tex="(-4, 0, 0)" />.

**Swapping flips it.** With <Formula tex="\vec{w}" /> first, your fingers swap and your thumb points the other way: <Formula tex="\vec{w} \times \vec{v} = -(\vec{v} \times \vec{w})" />. That's the 2D sign rule again: in 2D the "arrow" can only point out of the screen (positive) or into it (negative).

**Why does that strange formula do all this?** That's Lesson 5.3 (optional). For now, check property 2 with numbers: <Formula tex="(-4, 0, 0) \cdot (0, 0, 2) = 0" /> and <Formula tex="(-4, 0, 0) \cdot (0, 2, 0) = 0" />.

## Go deeper

**A way to remember the formula.** Write a "determinant" with <Formula tex="\hat{\imath}, \hat{\jmath}, \hat{k}" /> in the first column:

<Formula display tex="\vec{v} \times \vec{w} = \det \begin{bmatrix} \hat{\imath} & v_1 & w_1 \\ \hat{\jmath} & v_2 & w_2 \\ \hat{k} & v_3 & w_3 \end{bmatrix}" />

Expanding it gives the three parts. It isn't a real matrix (vectors inside a matrix of numbers!), but Lesson 5.3 explains why the trick works.

**Only in 3D.** In 2D the cross product is a number, not a vector. In 4D there are many directions at right angles to a parallelogram, so there is no single "out". The 3D cross product is special.

**Physics uses it everywhere**: turning force (torque = r × F), and the push a magnet gives a moving charge (F = q v × B).
