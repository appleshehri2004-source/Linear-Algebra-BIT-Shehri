## Question

A matrix moves almost every arrow **off** the line it started on: the arrow turns as well as stretches. Are there any arrows that only get stretched, and stay on their own line?

## Play

The yellow probe <Formula tex="\vec{v}" /> sweeps around the circle. The orange arrow is <Formula tex="A\vec{v}" />. Press **Sweep round**, or drag the probe yourself (it clicks onto a line when you get close).

Whenever <Formula tex="A\vec{v}" /> lands back on <Formula tex="\vec{v}" />'s dashed line, that line lights up in cyan and stays drawn, with its stretch factor λ.

Start with <Formula tex="A = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 1 \\ 0 & 2 \end{bmatrix}" />, then try the other three matrices. Last, press **Watch the whole plane move**.

## Numbers

Two lines light up for <Formula tex="A = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 1 \\ 0 & 2 \end{bmatrix}" />. Check them by multiplying:

<Formula display tex="\begin{bmatrix} 3 & 1 \\ 0 & 2 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} = 3 \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 1 \\ 0 & 2 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -2 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} = 2 \begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}" />

Any other arrow gets knocked off its line. For example <Formula tex="(0, 1)" /> goes to <Formula tex="(1, 2)" />, which points somewhere else.

## Explain

**Eigenvector and eigenvalue.** A non-zero arrow <Formula tex="\vec{v}" /> with

<Formula display tex="A\vec{v} = \lambda \vec{v}" />

is an **eigenvector** of <Formula tex="A" />, and the number λ is its **eigenvalue**: how much the arrow is stretched along its own line. λ = 3 triples it; λ = ½ halves it; a negative λ flips it to the other side of the origin (still on the same line).

**It's the line that counts.** If <Formula tex="\vec{v}" /> is an eigenvector, so is <Formula tex="2\vec{v}" />, <Formula tex="-\vec{v}" />, or any other multiple. That is why a whole line lights up, not just one arrow.

**The four matrices tell four stories:**

- **[3 1; 0 2]**: two lines, with λ = 3 and λ = 2.
- **Rotation by 90°**: no line ever lights up. A turn knocks **every** arrow off its line, so there are no (real) eigenvectors.
- **Shear**: just one line, the x-axis, with λ = 1. Every other arrow leans over.
- **2I**: every line lights up. Doubling everything keeps every arrow on its line, so every vector is an eigenvector with λ = 2.

**Watch the whole plane move.** In that view, the arrows sitting on cyan lines slide along them as the grid moves. Every other arrow swings to a new direction. The eigenvectors are the **axes** of the transformation: the directions where it is just a stretch.

## Predict

<Formula tex="A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \end{bmatrix}" /> stretches x by 2 and flips y. Which lines light up, and with which λ?

<details>
  <summary>Reveal</summary>
  The x-axis, with λ = 2: <Formula tex="(1, 0) \mapsto (2, 0)" />. And the y-axis, with λ = −1: <Formula tex="(0, 1) \mapsto (0, -1)" />. That arrow is flipped to the other side of the origin, but it stays on its line, so it counts. For a diagonal matrix, the axes are the eigenvectors and the diagonal numbers are the eigenvalues.
</details>

## Go deeper

**Why care?** An eigenvector turns a matrix problem into a number problem. Along that line, <Formula tex="A" /> is just "times λ". Apply <Formula tex="A" /> ten times, and an eigenvector is just multiplied by <Formula tex="\lambda^{10}" /> (Lesson 5.3.1).

**3D rotations have one.** A rotation in 3D always has an axis it spins around. That axis is an eigenvector with λ = 1: it doesn't move at all (Lesson 1.9.4).

**Rotation's eigenvalues aren't gone, they're complex.** For the 90° turn, the maths of the next lesson gives λ = i and λ = −i. No real arrow stays on its line, but complex numbers are, in a sense, "rotation numbers". That story continues in Chapter 7.
