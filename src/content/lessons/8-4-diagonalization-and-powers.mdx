What is <Formula tex="A^{100}" />? Multiplying a matrix by itself 100 times is a lot of work. Can eigenvectors make it easy?

## Numbers

<Formula tex="A = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 1 \\ 0 & 2 \end{bmatrix}" /> has eigenvectors <Formula tex="(1, 0)" /> (λ = 3) and <Formula tex="(-1, 1)" /> (λ = 2). Put them in the columns of <Formula tex="P" />, and the λs on the diagonal of <Formula tex="D" />:

<Formula display tex="A = P D P^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}" />

Then the middle <Formula tex="P^{-1}P" />s cancel in <Formula tex="A^n = (PDP^{-1})(PDP^{-1})\cdots" />, leaving

<Formula display tex="A^n = P D^n P^{-1}, \qquad D^{10} = \begin{bmatrix} 3^{10} & 0 \\ 0 & 2^{10} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 59049 & 0 \\ 0 & 1024 \end{bmatrix}" />

That gives exactly the same <Formula tex="A^{10}" /> as ten multiplications (the site's tests check it).

## Explain

**Read PDP⁻¹ right to left,** like Lesson 7.2: translate into eigen-numbers (<Formula tex="P^{-1}" />), stretch each axis by its λ (<Formula tex="D" />), translate back (<Formula tex="P" />). In its own eigenbasis, <Formula tex="A" /> is just the diagonal matrix <Formula tex="D" />. That is what **diagonalization** means.

**Powers are easy in the eigenbasis.** Applying <Formula tex="A" /> n times there is just stretching n times: the numbers become <Formula tex="(c_1\lambda_1^n,\; c_2\lambda_2^n)" />. That's what you saw in the eigenbasis view.

**The biggest λ wins.** As n grows, the part with the biggest <Formula tex="|\lambda|" /> takes over, so <Formula tex="A^n\vec{v}" /> swings round towards that eigen-line.

- **Fibonacci.** <Formula tex="\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}" /> turns <Formula tex="(F_{k+1}, F_k)" /> into <Formula tex="(F_{k+1} + F_k, F_{k+1})" />. Its eigenvalues are <Formula tex="\tfrac{1 \pm \sqrt{5}}{2}" />, about 1.618 and −0.618. The second one shrinks to nothing, so <Formula tex="F_n" /> grows like <Formula tex="1.618^n" />, and <Formula tex="F_{10} = 55" />.
- **Markov.** Each step, 10% of group 1 moves to group 2 and 20% of group 2 moves back. The eigenvalues are 1 and 0.7. <Formula tex="0.7^n \to 0" />, and λ = 1 leaves its eigenvector alone, so the split settles at <Formula tex="(2/3, 1/3)" />, whatever the start.

**Not every matrix can do this.** A shear has only one eigen-line, so there aren't enough eigenvectors to fill <Formula tex="P" />. A rotation has no real ones. That is why PowerView has no eigenbasis view for them.

## Predict

In the Markov example, start with everyone in group 2 instead, <Formula tex="\vec{v} = (0, 1)" />. Where does it settle?

## Go deeper

**A formula for Fibonacci.** Working out <Formula tex="P D^n P^{-1}" /> by hand gives Binet's formula, <Formula tex="F_n = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{5}}\left(\varphi^n - (1 - \varphi)^n\right)" /> with <Formula tex="\varphi = \tfrac{1 + \sqrt{5}}{2}" />. A whole number from square roots of 5!

**Google's PageRank** is a giant Markov matrix. The ranking of the web pages is its λ = 1 eigenvector, found by applying the matrix again and again, just like the dots in the Markov example.

**Similar matrices again.** <Formula tex="A = PDP^{-1}" /> says A and D are similar (Lesson 7.2): the same transformation in two languages. Eigenvectors are the language where it's simplest.
