## Question

A matrix stretches, squashes and slants the plane. Does every shape grow by a different amount, or is there **one number** that says how much every area changes?

## Numbers

**Stretch.** The columns are <Formula tex="(3, 0)" /> and <Formula tex="(0, 2)" />: <span class="ihat">**î**</span> becomes 3 long, <span class="jhat">**ĵ**</span> becomes 2 tall. The unit square becomes a 3 by 2 rectangle, so its area goes from 1 to <Formula tex="3 \times 2 = 6" />.

**Shear.** The columns are <Formula tex="(1, 0)" /> and <Formula tex="(1, 1)" />. The square slants into a parallelogram. Its base is still 1 and its height is still 1, so its area is still **1**. A shear moves things around without changing area.

**Shrink.** Halving both directions gives area <Formula tex="0.5 \times 0.5 = 0.25" />.

The blob's area changed by **the same factor** each time: × 6, × 1, × 0.25.

## Explain

**The determinant of a matrix is the number that every area gets multiplied by.** We write it <Formula tex="\det A" />. It's enough to watch the unit square: whatever happens to its area happens to every area.

**Why one number works for every shape.** Fill any shape with tiny grid squares. A linear transformation keeps grid lines straight, parallel and evenly spaced, so every tiny square becomes the same tiny parallelogram, scaled by the same amount as the unit square. Add up the tiny pieces and the whole shape is scaled by that amount too.

So for the stretch:

<Formula display tex="\det \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 \end{bmatrix} = 6 \qquad \det \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = 1" />

## Go deeper

A transformation with determinant 1 keeps every area exactly the same, even though shapes may look very different. Rotations and shears both have determinant 1. Can you find a matrix with determinant 1 that isn't a rotation or a shear? (Hint: stretch one way and squash the other.)
