## Numbers

Take the **Rank 1** matrix. Its second column is 2 copies of the first:

<Formula display tex="A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 4 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad A\begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \end{bmatrix} = x\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} + y\begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix} = (x + 2y)\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}" />

Every output is some number of copies of <Formula tex="(1, 2)" />, so every output is on the line through <Formula tex="(1, 2)" />. That line is the <span class="space">**column space**</span>.

The output is <Formula tex="\vec{0}" /> exactly when <Formula tex="x + 2y = 0" />. Take <Formula tex="y = 1" />, so <Formula tex="x = -2" />:

<Formula display tex="A\begin{bmatrix} -2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = -2\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} + 1\begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}" />

So the whole line through <Formula tex="(-2, 1)" /> lands on <Formula tex="\vec{0}" />. That line is the <span class="null">**null space**</span>.

In 3D, the **Rank 2** matrix has columns <Formula tex="(1, 2, 3)" />, <Formula tex="(2, 4, 6)" /> and <Formula tex="(0, 1, 1)" />. Column 2 is 2 copies of column 1, so it adds nothing new. Space lands on the plane made by columns 1 and 3, and the line through <Formula tex="(-2, 1, 0)" /> lands on <Formula tex="\vec{0}" />.

## Explain

The <span class="space">**column space**</span> is everywhere <Formula tex="A" /> can land: every mix of its columns.

The **rank** is how many dimensions the column space has. In 2D, rank 2 means the whole plane, rank 1 means a line, and rank 0 means just the origin. Elimination (Lesson 4.2) finds it: **the rank is the number of pivots.**

The <span class="null">**null space**</span> is everything <Formula tex="A" /> sends to <Formula tex="\vec{0}" />:

<Formula display tex="\text{null space} = \{\, \vec{x} : A\vec{x} = \vec{0} \,\}" />

It always goes through the origin, and if <Formula tex="\vec{x}" /> is in it, so is every multiple: <Formula tex="A(c\vec{x}) = c\,A\vec{x} = \vec{0}" />. That's why it is a line or a plane, never a curve.

**Squashing trades dimensions.** Every dimension the output loses becomes a dimension of the null space. For a matrix with <Formula tex="n" /> columns:

<Formula display tex="\text{rank} + \dim(\text{null space}) = n" />

Rank 2 in the plane: <Formula tex="2 + 0" />. Rank 1: <Formula tex="1 + 1" />. Rank 0: <Formula tex="0 + 2" />. In 3D, rank 2 is <Formula tex="2 + 1" /> and rank 1 is <Formula tex="1 + 2" />.

## Go deeper

**Rank and the determinant.** A square matrix has <Formula tex="\det A \neq 0" /> exactly when its rank is full (2 for 2×2, 3 for 3×3). Then nothing is squashed, and the null space is just <Formula tex="\vec{0}" />.

**Why the null space matters for solving.** If <Formula tex="A\vec{n} = \vec{0}" /> and <Formula tex="A\vec{x} = \vec{b}" />, then <Formula tex="A(\vec{x} + \vec{n}) = \vec{b}" /> too. A bigger null space means more solutions. That's the next lesson.

**Other names.** The null space is also called the **kernel**, and the column space the **image** or **range**.
