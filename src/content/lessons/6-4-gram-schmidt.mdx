## Numbers

Start with <Formula tex="\vec{v}_1 = (1, 1)" /> and <Formula tex="\vec{v}_2 = (1, 0)" />.

**Step 1.** Keep <Formula tex="\vec{v}_1" />. The shadow of <Formula tex="\vec{v}_2" /> on it is <Formula tex="\frac{1}{2}(1, 1) = (\tfrac{1}{2}, \tfrac{1}{2})" />.

**Step 2.** <Formula tex="\vec{w}_2 = (1, 0) - (\tfrac{1}{2}, \tfrac{1}{2}) = (\tfrac{1}{2}, -\tfrac{1}{2})" />. Check: <Formula tex="(1, 1) \cdot (\tfrac{1}{2}, -\tfrac{1}{2}) = 0" />. ✓

**Step 3.** Divide each by its length:

<Formula display tex="\vec{q}_1 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \vec{q}_2 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad Q = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad Q^T Q = I" />

The shear <Formula tex="\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}" /> sends ĵ to <Formula tex="(1, 1)" />, which is <Formula tex="\sqrt{2} \approx 1.41" /> long. Its columns have dot product <Formula tex="(1, 0) \cdot (1, 1) = 1" />, not 0. So it is not orthogonal.

## Explain

**Gram–Schmidt is projection, over and over.** Each new arrow loses its parts along the arrows already made (Lesson 6.2). What is left is perpendicular to all of them. Dividing by the length makes it 1 long. With three arrows in 3D, the third loses its shadows on both <Formula tex="\vec{q}_1" /> and <Formula tex="\vec{q}_2" />.

**The span doesn't change.** <Formula tex="\vec{w}_2" /> is <Formula tex="\vec{v}_2" /> minus a copy of <Formula tex="\vec{v}_1" />, so it still lies in the same plane. Gram–Schmidt only straightens the grid. It doesn't move the space.

**Orthogonal matrices.** A square matrix <Formula tex="Q" /> whose columns are 1 long and at right angles is called orthogonal. Then <Formula tex="Q^T Q = I" />, so <Formula tex="Q^{-1} = Q^T" />: undoing it is just a transpose.

**They keep lengths and angles.** <Formula tex="(Q\vec{v}) \cdot (Q\vec{w}) = \vec{v}^T Q^T Q \vec{w} = \vec{v} \cdot \vec{w}" />. Dot products stay the same, and lengths and angles are made from dot products. Rotations and flips are orthogonal. A shear is not: its square bends into a slanted diamond.

## Go deeper

**A = QR.** Gram–Schmidt writes any matrix with independent columns as <Formula tex="A = QR" />: <Formula tex="Q" /> holds the clean arrows, and <Formula tex="R" /> (upper triangular) records how to rebuild the old arrows from them. Computers use this to solve least squares, because it avoids <Formula tex="A^T A" />.

**Projections get easy.** With an orthonormal basis, the shadow of <Formula tex="\vec{v}" /> is just <Formula tex="(\vec{q}_1 \cdot \vec{v})\,\vec{q}_1 + (\vec{q}_2 \cdot \vec{v})\,\vec{q}_2" />. There are no normal equations to solve. That is exactly how the 3D demo in Lesson 6.2 works.

**Determinant ±1.** An orthogonal matrix keeps areas too, so its determinant is 1 (a rotation) or −1 (a flip).
