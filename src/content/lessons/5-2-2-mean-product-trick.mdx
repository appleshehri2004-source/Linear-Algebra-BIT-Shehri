## Question

Writing out <Formula tex="\det(A - \lambda I)" /> and solving a quadratic every time is slow. Can you read the eigenvalues of a 2×2 matrix almost straight off the matrix?

## Play

Type any 2×2 matrix, or load one of the examples. Watch the steps: **m** comes from the diagonal, **p** is the determinant, and then the eigenvalues drop out.

Try to work out the answer in your head before reading the last line.

## Numbers

For <Formula tex="A = \begin{bmatrix} 8 & 4 \\ 2 & 6 \end{bmatrix}" />:

<Formula display tex="m = \frac{8 + 6}{2} = 7, \qquad p = 8 \cdot 6 - 4 \cdot 2 = 40, \qquad \lambda = 7 \pm \sqrt{49 - 40} = 7 \pm 3" />

So λ = 10 and λ = 4. Check: their mean is 7 and their product is 40 ✓

For <Formula tex="\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 7 \\ 1 & 8 \end{bmatrix}" />: m = 5, p = 16 − 7 = 9, λ = 5 ± √16 = 5 ± 4, so 9 and 1.

## Explain

**Two facts about the eigenvalues** λ₁ and λ₂ of any 2×2 matrix:

1. They **add up to the trace** (the diagonal added up), so their **mean** is <Formula tex="m = \tfrac{1}{2}\,\text{trace}" />.
2. They **multiply to the determinant**: <Formula tex="p = \lambda_1 \lambda_2 = \det A" />. (That fits Chapter 3: stretching by λ₁ one way and λ₂ the other scales areas by λ₁λ₂.)

Both come from the characteristic polynomial: <Formula tex="\lambda^2 - (\text{trace})\lambda + \det = (\lambda - \lambda_1)(\lambda - \lambda_2)" />. Multiply out the right side and compare.

**Two numbers from their mean and product.** The two numbers sit the same distance d either side of m: <Formula tex="\lambda = m \pm d" />. Their product is <Formula tex="(m + d)(m - d) = m^2 - d^2 = p" />, so <Formula tex="d = \sqrt{m^2 - p}" />:

<Formula display tex="\lambda = m \pm \sqrt{m^2 - p}" />

**When m² − p is negative,** there is no real square root, so there are no real eigenvalues. Load the rotation example: m = 0, p = 1, and <Formula tex="\sqrt{-1}" /> gives λ = ±i.

## Predict

<Formula tex="A = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 1 \\ 4 & 1 \end{bmatrix}" />. Will the eigenvalues be whole numbers?

<details>
  <summary>Reveal</summary>
  No. m = 2, p = 3 − 4 = −1, so λ = 2 ± √(4 + 1) = 2 ± √5: about 4.24 and −0.24. Whole-number eigenvalues only happen when m² − p is a perfect square (and m is a whole number or a half).
</details>

## Go deeper

**Quick checks.** Even when you find eigenvalues another way, trace and det check your answer: the eigenvalues must add to the trace and multiply to det.

**Triangular matrices.** For <Formula tex="\begin{bmatrix} a & b \\ 0 & d \end{bmatrix}" />, p = ad and m = (a + d)/2, so the eigenvalues are just a and d. The diagonal of a triangular matrix **is** its eigenvalues (Lesson 5.1.1's first matrix).

**Bigger matrices.** The sum of the eigenvalues is still the trace and their product is still the determinant, in any size. But "mean and product" are no longer enough to pin them down once there are three or more.
