## Question

A matrix moves the whole plane. Can you always move it **back**? And if you can, how does that solve <Formula tex="A\vec{x} = \vec{v}" />?

## Numbers

The **shear** pushes <span class="jhat">**ĵ**</span> one step to the right. To undo it, push it one step back to the left:

<Formula display tex="A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad A^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}" />

<Formula display tex="A^{-1}A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = I" />

**Rotate 90°** is undone by rotating 90° the other way. The matrix from **Lesson 4.1** has <Formula tex="\det A = 3" />, and its inverse is full of thirds:

<Formula display tex="A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}" />

<Formula display tex="A^{-1} = \frac13 \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ -1 & 2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1/3 & 1/3 \\ -1/3 & 2/3 \end{bmatrix}" />

## Explain

The **inverse** <Formula tex="A^{-1}" /> is the matrix that undoes <Formula tex="A" />. Doing <Formula tex="A" /> and then <Formula tex="A^{-1}" /> changes nothing:

<Formula display tex="A^{-1}A = I" />

For a 2×2 matrix there's a formula: swap <Formula tex="a" /> and <Formula tex="d" />, change the signs of <Formula tex="b" /> and <Formula tex="c" />, and divide by the determinant.

<Formula display tex="\begin{bmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{bmatrix}^{-1} = \frac{1}{ad - bc} \begin{bmatrix} d & -b \\ -c & a \end{bmatrix}" />

**Why divide by the determinant?** <Formula tex="A" /> multiplies every area by <Formula tex="\det A" />, so undoing it must multiply every area by <Formula tex="1 / \det A" />.

**When det = 0 there is no inverse.** Press **Squash**: the whole plane lands on the x-axis. The two orange dots, <Formula tex="(2, 1)" /> and <Formula tex="(2, -2)" />, both land on <Formula tex="(2, 0)" />. To undo that, <Formula tex="(2, 0)" /> would have to go back to two places at once. **You can't unsquish a line.** And the formula agrees: it would divide by 0.

**Solving with the inverse.** Rewind both sides of <Formula tex="A\vec{x} = \vec{v}" /> with <Formula tex="A^{-1}" />:

<Formula display tex="A\vec{x} = \vec{v} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \vec{x} = A^{-1}\vec{v}" />

For Lesson 4.1's system, with <Formula tex="\vec{v} = (1, 5)" />:

<Formula display tex="\vec{x} = \frac13 \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ -1 & 2 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 5 \end{bmatrix} = \frac13 \begin{bmatrix} 6 \\ 9 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix}" />

That's the same answer as the game in Lesson 4.1.

## Go deeper

**Undo in reverse order.** If you put on socks and then shoes, you take off the shoes first. In the same way, <Formula tex="(AB)^{-1} = B^{-1}A^{-1}" />.

**Computers rarely use the inverse.** To solve <Formula tex="A\vec{x} = \vec{v}" />, elimination (Lesson 4.2) is faster and more accurate than working out <Formula tex="A^{-1}" /> first. The inverse is the right *idea*, and elimination is the right *method*. In fact, elimination on <Formula tex="[A \mid I]" /> is how <Formula tex="A^{-1}" /> is found for bigger matrices.

**Bigger matrices.** A 3×3 matrix has an inverse exactly when its determinant isn't 0: it doesn't squash space onto a plane or a line.
