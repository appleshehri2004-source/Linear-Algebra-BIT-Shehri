## Question

Turning, stretching, slanting, flipping, squashing: what does each of these look like as a matrix?

## Numbers

To build a matrix, ask "where do <span class="ihat">**î**</span> and <span class="jhat">**ĵ**</span> land?" and write the answers as columns.

| Transformation | î lands on | ĵ lands on | Matrix |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Rotate 90° anticlockwise | <Formula tex="(0, 1)" /> | <Formula tex="(-1, 0)" /> | <Formula tex="\begin{bmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}" /> |
| Shear | <Formula tex="(1, 0)" /> | <Formula tex="(1, 1)" /> | <Formula tex="\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}" /> |
| Scale by 3 and 2 | <Formula tex="(3, 0)" /> | <Formula tex="(0, 2)" /> | <Formula tex="\begin{bmatrix} 3 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 \end{bmatrix}" /> |
| Reflect over the y-axis | <Formula tex="(-1, 0)" /> | <Formula tex="(0, 1)" /> | <Formula tex="\begin{bmatrix} -1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}" /> |
| Project onto the x-axis | <Formula tex="(1, 0)" /> | <Formula tex="(0, 0)" /> | <Formula tex="\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}" /> |

For example, the shear sends <Formula tex="(2, 3)" /> to <Formula tex="2 \cdot (1, 0) + 3 \cdot (1, 1) = (5, 3)" />: each point slides sideways by its height.

## Explain

- **Rotate 90°:** î turns up to <Formula tex="(0, 1)" />, ĵ turns left to <Formula tex="(-1, 0)" />. Lengths and angles stay the same.
- **Shear:** î stays put, ĵ leans over. Horizontal lines slide sideways; the higher the line, the further it slides. Squares become slanted parallelograms with the same area.
- **Scale:** î and ĵ stay on their axes but get longer. Everything stretches 3 times wider and 2 times taller.
- **Reflect:** î flips to <Formula tex="(-1, 0)" />. The plane turns over like a page, so a clockwise loop becomes anticlockwise.
- **Project:** ĵ is squashed to <Formula tex="\vec{0}" />, so the whole plane collapses onto the x-axis. Each point drops straight down (or up) onto it.
- **Mystery:** columns <Formula tex="(1, 2)" /> and <Formula tex="(3, 1)" />. It doesn't have a simple name, and that's fine: most matrices are a mix of stretching, slanting and turning.

## Predict

After **Project**, can you get the original plane back? Is there a transformation that undoes it?

<details>
  <summary>Reveal</summary>
  No. <Formula tex="(2, 1)" />, <Formula tex="(2, 5)" /> and <Formula tex="(2, -3)" /> all land on the same point <Formula tex="(2, 0)" />. Once they're squashed together, no rule can tell them apart again. The other five can all be undone (try finding the matrix that undoes each one).
</details>

## Go deeper

Rotating by any angle <Formula tex="\theta" /> sends î to <Formula tex="(\cos\theta, \sin\theta)" /> and ĵ to <Formula tex="(-\sin\theta, \cos\theta)" />, so its matrix is

<Formula display tex="\begin{bmatrix} \cos\theta & -\sin\theta \\ \sin\theta & \cos\theta \end{bmatrix}" />

Put <Formula tex="\theta = 90°" /> in and you get the first row of the table. Whether a matrix can be undone, and by how much it changes area, are the next two big ideas: the *inverse* and the *determinant*.
