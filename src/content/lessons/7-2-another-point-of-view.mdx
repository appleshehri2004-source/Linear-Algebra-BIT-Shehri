## Question

We write "turn everything 90° anticlockwise" as the matrix <Formula tex="M = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}" />. Jennifer wants the **same** turn, but she thinks in her numbers. What matrix should **she** write?

## Numbers

Follow her arrow <Formula tex="(1, 2)" /> through the three steps:

<Formula display tex="\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix}}_{\text{1. into our numbers}} \qquad \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -3 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}}_{\text{2. turn 90°}} \qquad \underbrace{\tfrac{1}{3} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ -1 & 2 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} -3 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}}_{\text{3. back to hers}}" />

Multiply the three matrices together (right to left, Lesson 2.4) and you get her matrix:

<Formula display tex="P^{-1} M P = \begin{bmatrix} 1/3 & -2/3 \\ 5/3 & -1/3 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \begin{bmatrix} 1/3 & -2/3 \\ 5/3 & -1/3 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1/3 - 4/3 \\ 5/3 - 2/3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}" />

Same answer, <Formula tex="(-1, 1)" />, in one step.

## Explain

**Read P⁻¹MP from right to left**, like any chain of matrices:

1. <Formula tex="P" />: "here's an arrow in Jennifer's numbers; say it in ours".
2. <Formula tex="M" />: "do the transformation, in our numbers, where we know how".
3. <Formula tex="P^{-1}" />: "say the answer in Jennifer's numbers again".

Multiplied together, the three steps are one matrix. It does the same turn, but it speaks Jennifer's language: her numbers in, her numbers out.

**Same transformation, different matrix.** <Formula tex="M" /> and <Formula tex="P^{-1} M P" /> look nothing alike. One has 0s and 1s; the other has thirds. But they describe **the same movement of space**, each in its own language. Matrices like this are called **similar**.

**The columns still mean something.** The first column of her matrix, <Formula tex="(1/3, 5/3)" />, is where her b₁ lands after the turn, written in her numbers. Check: turning <Formula tex="(2, 1)" /> gives <Formula tex="(-1, 2)" />, and <Formula tex="\tfrac{1}{3}(2, 1) + \tfrac{5}{3}(-1, 1) = (-1, 2)" />. ✓

## Go deeper

**What similar matrices share.** <Formula tex="M" /> and <Formula tex="P^{-1}MP" /> are the same movement, so anything about the movement itself agrees. Both multiply areas by the same number: <Formula tex="\det(P^{-1}MP) = \det M" /> (Lesson 3.4). For our example both are 1.

**Picking the best language.** The point of changing basis is often to find the grid where a transformation is simplest. When that grid is made of eigenvectors, her matrix has numbers only on the diagonal: pure stretching. That's the idea behind eigenvalues.

**Order matters.** <Formula tex="P M P^{-1}" /> is a different matrix. It goes the other way: it translates **our** numbers into Jennifer's, does <Formula tex="M" /> as if <Formula tex="M" /> were written in **her** numbers, and translates back. Getting the order wrong is the most common mistake here, so always ask: which language goes in, and which comes out?
