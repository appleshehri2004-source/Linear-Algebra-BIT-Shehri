## Question

Every matrix so far was square: 2 by 2 or 3 by 3. What could a matrix with 3 rows and 2 columns do? Or 1 row and 2 columns?

## Numbers

The 3 by 2 matrix has two columns, one for <span class="ihat">**î**</span> and one for <span class="jhat">**ĵ**</span>. Each column has three numbers, because each one lands in 3D:

<Formula display tex="\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ -1 & 1 \\ -2 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = 1 \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ -1 \\ -2 \end{bmatrix} + 1 \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 0 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix}" />

The 1 by 2 matrix also has two columns, but each column is just one number: <span class="ihat">**î**</span> lands on 1 and <span class="jhat">**ĵ**</span> lands on −2.

<Formula display tex="\begin{bmatrix} 1 & -2 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 4 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix} = 4 \cdot 1 + 3 \cdot (-2) = -2" />

## Explain

- **Columns = how many numbers go in.** There is one column for each basis arrow of the input. 2 columns means the input is 2D.
- **Rows = how many numbers come out.** Each column says where a basis arrow lands, written with as many numbers as the output has. 3 rows means the output is 3D.
- **Same rule as always.** Take <Formula tex="x" /> copies of column 1 plus <Formula tex="y" /> copies of column 2. Nothing about "multiply by the columns" needs the matrix to be square.
- **A 3 by 2 matrix can't fill 3D.** Every output is built from just two columns, so all of them lie on one flat sheet through the origin. That sheet is the *column space*: the span of the columns, like in lesson 1.3.
- **A 1 by 2 matrix is the dot product in disguise.** <Formula tex="\begin{bmatrix} 1 & -2 \end{bmatrix} \vec{v}" /> is the same sum as <Formula tex="(1, -2) \cdot \vec{v}" /> from lesson 1.5. Every point on a line at right angles to <Formula tex="(1, -2)" /> lands on the same number.

## Predict

Which points of the plane land on **0** on the number line under <Formula tex="\begin{bmatrix} 1 & -2 \end{bmatrix}" />?

## Go deeper

A matrix with <Formula tex="m" /> rows and <Formula tex="n" /> columns (an "<Formula tex="m \times n" /> matrix") takes <Formula tex="n" />-dimensional vectors to <Formula tex="m" />-dimensional ones. Rows first, then columns, the same order as a seat number in a theatre.

To multiply two matrices, you do one move and then the other, so the sizes have to fit: the output of the first must be the input of the second. A <Formula tex="1 \times 3" /> matrix can follow a <Formula tex="3 \times 2" /> one (2D to 3D, then 3D to a number), and the product is <Formula tex="1 \times 2" />. Try to explain why a <Formula tex="3 \times 2" /> matrix can't follow another <Formula tex="3 \times 2" /> one.
