## Question

<Formula tex="2x^2 + 2xy + 2y^2" /> gives every point <Formula tex="(x, y)" /> one number. What shape does it make? It's a bowl. But the <Formula tex="2xy" /> part tilts it. Which way does it point, and how do you see that without drawing it?

## Numbers

A **symmetric** matrix is the same after you swap rows and columns: <Formula tex="A^T = A" />, so its top-right number equals its bottom-left one. For <Formula tex="A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}" />:

<Formula display tex="\vec{x}^T A \vec{x} = \begin{bmatrix} x & y \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 2x + y \\ x + 2y \end{bmatrix} = 2x^2 + 2xy + 2y^2" />

So <Formula tex="(1, 0)" /> gives 2 and <Formula tex="(1, 1)" /> gives <Formula tex="2 + 2 + 2 = 6" />. The eigenvalues are 3 along <Formula tex="(1, 1)" /> and 1 along <Formula tex="(1, -1)" />, and those two directions are at right angles: <Formula tex="(1, 1) \cdot (1, -1) = 0" />.

## Explain

**Symmetric matrices have the best eigenvectors.** Their eigenvalues are always real, and their eigenvectors are always at right angles. (Lesson 8.3's rotation, which had none, is not symmetric.) Make the eigenvectors 1 long and put them in <Formula tex="Q" />, an orthogonal matrix (Lesson 6.4), so <Formula tex="Q^{-1} = Q^T" />:

<Formula display tex="A = Q \Lambda Q^T" />

**In the eigenvectors' own numbers the cross term disappears.** Write <Formula tex="\vec{x} = p\,\vec{q}_1 + q\,\vec{q}_2" />. Then

<Formula display tex="\vec{x}^T A \vec{x} = \lambda_1 p^2 + \lambda_2 q^2 = 3p^2 + q^2" />

No <Formula tex="pq" /> term. That's why the eigenvectors are the axes of the curves: along each axis the value only grows like <Formula tex="\lambda t^2" />. That's the cyan curve on the 3D surface, steepest along <Formula tex="\lambda_1" />.

**The signs of the λs give the shape.**

- Both above 0 (**positive definite**): every <Formula tex="\vec{x} \neq \vec{0}" /> gives a positive number. A bowl, with ellipse level curves. The curve <Formula tex="\vec{x}^T A \vec{x} = 1" /> reaches <Formula tex="1/\sqrt{\lambda}" /> along each eigenvector, so a big λ makes a short axis.
- One above, one below (**indefinite**): up along one eigenvector, down along the other. A saddle, with hyperbolas.
- One is 0 (**semidefinite**): flat along that eigenvector. A trough, with straight-line level curves.

For a 2×2 there's a shortcut: λ₁λ₂ = det A. If det A > 0 and the top-left number is above 0, it's a bowl. If det A < 0, it's a saddle.

## Predict

<Formula tex="A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix}" /> has det = 1 − 4 = −3. Bowl or saddle? And is <Formula tex="\vec{x}^T A \vec{x}" /> positive or negative at <Formula tex="(1, -1)" />?

<details>
  <summary>Reveal</summary>
  A saddle: det A < 0, so the eigenvalues (3 and −1) have opposite signs. <Formula tex="(1, -1)" /> is the λ = −1 eigenvector, so the value there is <Formula tex="-1 \cdot |(1, -1)|^2 = -2" />. Check: <Formula tex="1 - 4 + 1 = -2" />. Press **Saddle** and drag <Formula tex="\vec{x}" /> there.

## Go deeper

**Minimum or not?** In calculus with two variables, the second derivatives of a function at a flat point make a symmetric matrix (the Hessian). Positive definite means a minimum, indefinite means a saddle point. It's the same bowl-or-saddle question.

**The spectral theorem** says every symmetric matrix, of any size, is <Formula tex="Q \Lambda Q^T" />: real eigenvalues and perpendicular eigenvectors. Physics uses this everywhere, from the axes a spinning object turns about most easily to the main directions of stress in a beam.

**Every <Formula tex="A^T A" /> is symmetric and never negative**: <Formula tex="\vec{x}^T A^T A \vec{x} = |A\vec{x}|^2 \geq 0" />. The next lesson uses exactly this to take apart *any* matrix.
