## Question

In Lesson 1.5 the dot product was "multiply matching parts, then add". In Lesson 2.6 a 1×2 matrix squashed the plane onto a number line. Why do those give the **same** numbers? And why is the dot product connected to dropping one arrow onto another's line?

## Numbers

With <Formula tex="\hat{u} = (0.6, 0.8)" /> and <Formula tex="\vec{v} = (4, 3)" />, <span class="vec">**v**</span> lands at **4.8**. The dot product:

<Formula display tex="\hat{u} \cdot \vec{v} = 0.6 \times 4 + 0.8 \times 3 = 2.4 + 2.4 = 4.8" />

Now look at <span class="ihat">**î**</span> and <span class="jhat">**ĵ**</span>. <span class="ihat">**î**</span> lands at 0.6 and <span class="jhat">**ĵ**</span> lands at 0.8: **exactly û's two numbers**. So the squashing is the 1×2 matrix

<Formula display tex="\begin{bmatrix} 0.6 & 0.8 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 4 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix} = 0.6 \times 4 + 0.8 \times 3 = 4.8" />

That matrix is just <span class="vec2">**û**</span> tipped on its side. With <Formula tex="k = 3" />, <Formula tex="\vec{u} = (1.8, 2.4)" /> and

<Formula display tex="\begin{bmatrix} 1.8 & 2.4 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 4 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix} = 7.2 + 7.2 = 14.4 = 3 \times 4.8" />

## Explain

**Step 1: dropping onto the line is a linear transformation.** Grid lines stay evenly spaced on the number line and the origin lands on 0. So, like every linear transformation (Lesson 2.6), it is a matrix. Its output is one number, so it is a **1×2 matrix**, and its two columns are where <span class="ihat">**î**</span> and <span class="jhat">**ĵ**</span> land.

**Step 2: where does î land? Use symmetry.** <span class="ihat">**î**</span> and <span class="vec2">**û**</span> are both 1 long. Dropping <span class="ihat">**î**</span> onto <span class="vec2">**û**</span>'s line is the mirror image of dropping <span class="vec2">**û**</span> onto the x-axis (fold the picture along the line halfway between them). Dropping <span class="vec2">**û**</span> onto the x-axis gives its x part, <Formula tex="u_x" />. So <span class="ihat">**î**</span> lands at <Formula tex="u_x" />. The same with <span class="jhat">**ĵ**</span> gives <Formula tex="u_y" />.

**Step 3: put it together.** The matrix is <Formula tex="\begin{bmatrix} u_x & u_y \end{bmatrix}" />, and multiplying by it is <Formula tex="u_x v_x + u_y v_y" />: the dot product.

<Formula display tex="\text{(where } \vec{v} \text{ lands on } \hat{u}\text{'s line)} = \begin{bmatrix} u_x & u_y \end{bmatrix} \vec{v} = \hat{u} \cdot \vec{v}" />

**This is duality:** every 1×2 matrix (a way of squashing the plane onto a number line) is a vector lying on its side, and every vector is such a matrix. A longer <Formula tex="\vec{u} = k\hat{u}" /> means "drop onto the line, then multiply by k". That's why <Formula tex="\vec{u} \cdot \vec{v} = |\vec{u}|\,(\text{the shadow of } \vec{v})" />, the picture from Lesson 1.5.

## Go deeper

**It works in any size.** A 1×3 matrix squashes 3D space onto a number line, and it is a 3D vector on its side. Lesson 5.3 uses exactly this to explain the cross product.

**Rows are vectors in disguise.** Each row of a bigger matrix is a 1×n matrix, so each number of <Formula tex="A\vec{x}" /> is a dot product of a row with <Formula tex="\vec{x}" />. That's the "row picture" of Lesson 4.1, and why the null space is at right angles to every row (Lesson 4.7).

**The word "dual".** Mathematicians call the 1×n matrices the **dual space**. In flat space with the usual dot product, the dual space is the same as the space of vectors. In curved spaces (like in relativity) the two come apart, and keeping them apart matters.
